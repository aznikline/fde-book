Every book on methodology, at its close, must address one question: once you have mastered this method, where are the boundaries? The topic of this book is heavier than most methodology. Because what an FDE holds in hand is not ordinary technology—**it is the deepest secrets of the client's organization, and an ever-growing power to make decisions in place of people.**

The nature of FDE work determines what it will see. To deliver well, you must look at the client's most real operating data—including the ugly parts; you must map the organization's power structure—including who is incompetent, who is losing ground; you must touch the core business processes—including the shortcuts that skirt the gray zone. The client opens all of this to you on a plain understanding: you are here to help. This trust is the foundation of the FDE model, and destroying it takes only a single overstep.

I believe the professional ethics of FDE has at least six bottom lines. I set them down here as mutual encouragement with all my peers.

**First, the sovereignty of data belongs to the client.** Not a single byte of data seen at the client site should appear where it ought not to appear—not in AI training data (unless the contract explicitly authorizes it), not in case material (unless the client gives written consent), not as conversation fodder for your next job. The principle of minimizing data access is not just a technical norm, it is professional conduct: what need not be seen is not seen; what can be masked is masked.

**Second, report results honestly, including bad news.** Under outcome-based pricing, the gravest moral hazard is repackaging results—dressing up "the system went live" as "value was realized," dressing up correlation as causation. The value measurement system from Chapter 6 can be either the most honest tool or the most elaborate lie machine, the difference lies only in the human heart. The standing ground of FDE is "dare to be examined," so when the examined result is not good, you must present it with the same posture.

**Third, do not manufacture dependence, do not sell fear.** Two hidden evils exist in this industry: one is deliberately making the system a black box, so the client can never leave you; the other is exaggerating the panic of "you will die without AI" to close a deal. Gartner predicts that by 2028, 70% of enterprises will be forced to abandon frontline-deployment-led solutions due to cost and skill hollowing-out—this prediction is an alarm bell for the entire industry. A healthy FDE model should be "finish and leave": knowledge is transferred to the client, capability is deposited in the client team. **Make the client strong, not make the client addicted.**

**Fourth, take "the people being replaced" seriously.** The systems FDE delivers do, in many scenarios, genuinely replace part of people's work. This book covers the techniques of "change management" extensively, but beyond technique is ethics: do not celebrate efficiency in front of those being replaced, do not write people into a plan as a "cost item" without offering a way out, do not play dumb about "whose fate you are changing." The neutrality of technology is a myth; the deployer's choice is reality.

**Fifth, say no to "what the client asks for but should not be done."** You will encounter requests that skirt the edge of compliance: "help us make employee behavior monitoring a little finer," "this data is a bit fuzzy on compliance, but let's take it on first and talk later." The "French waiter" metaphor carries its deepest meaning here—true professionalism is not satisfying every request of the client, but daring to guide the client toward what is genuinely good for them and harmless to the world. The confidence to say no comes from having other clients on your books; so ethics is always also tied to the business model.

**Sixth, remember that you represent "technology" itself.** For many clients, you are the first human face they encounter of artificial intelligence. Every exaggeration of yours overdraws the credit of the entire industry in their minds; every promise kept makes a deposit for the entire industry. As artificial intelligence enters ever more deeply into the workings of society, the deployer is the last translator between technology and the human everyday—the cost of mistranslation is paid by everyone.

Palantir as a company is itself full of controversy: its history of serving intelligence and military institutions makes many people wary of everything about it—including the FDE model. My extensive citation of its methodology in this book does not amount to endorsing all of its client choices. Precisely the opposite—because the domains it serves are so sensitive, the discipline around permissions, audit, and least knowledge in its engineering culture is all the more worth borrowing from. **The greater the deployer's capability, the more the boundary must be written ahead of the method.**

FDE is a young profession, and no one has yet written its code of conduct. May this book be its first boundary marker.
