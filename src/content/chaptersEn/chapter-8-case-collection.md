The methodology of the first seven chapters was all distilled from real scenes of practice. This chapter restores those scenes in full: five sets of cases, from the inventors of the pattern to its latest inheritors, from Silicon Valley to China. When reading the cases, it helps to carry one question back to the first seven chapters: before the day these methods were named, how were they done by intuition?

## 8.1 Palantir: Twenty Years to Forge a "Clumsy Method" into a Moat

### Founding: Building Software for "Users You Cannot Ask"

In 2003, Silicon Valley was still lying in the wreckage of the dot-com bust. Peter Thiel pulled together a group of young Stanford alumni to do something that sounded insane: build data analysis software for the U.S. intelligence community. The company's name was taken from the *Lord of the Rings*—Palantir, the Seeing Stone that could see across distant lands.

The madness was not in the technology, but in the users. Bob McGrew's widely circulated recollection, years later, laid out the predicament most plainly: "One challenge of building software for spies is: I don't know any spies. You probably don't either. Even if you happen to find a spy and ask 'how do you actually work day to day,' he usually won't tell you."

The entire methodology of the software industry—user interviews, surveys, usability testing—collectively failed in front of this user group. Stephen Cohen, one of the co-founders, responded with something endearingly clumsy: build a demo sample, show it to people at the intelligence agency, and ask what they thought. The answer was merciless: "This is terrible. It has nothing to do with what we do." Cohen didn't retreat; he followed up: "So what would you want to be different?" Then he wrote down every opinion, took it back, revised it, and brought it back to their door. Loop, repeat.

This loop is the genetic fragment of FDE, and it contains two intuitions later proven worth a fortune: customers in complex domains, before they see something that works, do not know what they want; and the fastest way to learn "what they want" is to stand the builder next to the user.

### Taking Shape: From Emergency Measure to Organizational Strategy

Early Palantir walked the path of "one customer, one set of customization," and soon ran into the problem every enterprise software company runs into: what the second customer wanted was "subtly but critically different" from the first.

The standard solution is to extract commonalities, build a generic product, and say no to variation. But Palantir's customers didn't accept "no"—they were the CIA, they were the U.S. military on the battlefield. The man who led the company out of this dead end was early employee Shyam Sankar (later president and CTO). His solution was considered heresy at the time: neither build two products nor build one rigid product, but build a highly customizable platform and deploy engineers to the customer site to close the last mile.

Sankar's most consequential move was a rebellion on the ledger. In the software industry's financial language, "building customization for a single customer" is called services cost—the enemy of margin, to be minimized. Sankar redefined it as "product discovery"—on-site customization is not spending money, it's gathering intelligence for the product's next evolution. A single noun's replacement rewrote a company's capital allocation logic: what others saw as a cost center became Palantir's R&D center.

The organizational form took shape accordingly. Forward Deployed Engineers (internal codename "Delta") embedded with customers, "one customer, mobilizing many capabilities"; Deployment Strategists (codename "Echo") were responsible for reading the customer's mission and organization; platform engineers stayed in the rear, "one capability, serving many customers." By 2016, Palantir's Forward Deployed Engineers outnumbered platform engineers—a software company with the bulk of its forces at the "front line."

### Trial by Fire: Battlefields, Hurricanes, and Oil Fields

The value of the FDE pattern was proven over and over in one extreme scene after another.

On the battlefields of Iraq and Afghanistan, roadside bombs were the patrol's number-one killer. The deployed Palantir engineers found that soldiers had zero interest in fancy intelligence charts—all they wanted was to "mark this road as suspect on a map." The engineers threw together a crude map tool on the spot: a soldier clicks once, and the risky stretch becomes visible to the whole unit. This tool directly reduced casualties, and later settled into a standard feature of the platform. Note: it could not have been born in any headquarters conference room; it could only be born in the instant when an engineer and a soldier looked at the same road together.

The prototype scene of this playbook predated the map tool. Around 2007, Sankar led a small team into a classified information room at the U.S. counter-roadside-bomb operations center for two weeks of co-location. Inside the classified room, even speakerphone was forbidden; he used an elastic band to strap the phone to his head, freeing both hands to code—one ear taking the analyst's feedback, the other ear hearing colleagues back at Silicon Valley HQ. Nineteen hours a day: demo, ingest data, collect feedback, fix on the spot.

At the end of the two weeks, the analysts said: this thing is useful. Sankar himself was spent; he called Karp: "This is unsustainable, we're done." Karp's answer later became company doctrine: turn this "unsustainable" into an institution. (Source: Appendix C.)

At the 2012 Hurricane Sandy relief site, Palantir's Forward Deployed Engineers stitched scattered rescue data into a real-time map to coordinate the delivery of relief resources. Oil-drilling platforms, aircraft final-assembly workshops, bank trading floors—engineers took "on-site deployment" to a depth the software industry had never seen. Former Palantir engineer Barry recalls that the core components of the Foundry platform were born in Zurich, Houston, São Paulo, Toulouse, Baku—a world map where every point was an on-site act of creation.

The costs were equally real. Barry's record is a must-read counterexample for understanding this pattern: top engineers, global travel, massive reinvention of the wheel, free pilots, "many projects had a literal negative-infinity profit margin"; in 2014, the company's product strategy was literally "strong opinions, loosely held," chaotic enough to keep equity-holding employees up at night. Palantir could swallow these costs on the strength of two things: capital thickness propped up by sky-high contract values, and a "treat pilots like venture bets" mentality—it didn't matter if most bets went to zero, the ones that won had to pay for all of it.

Commercialization died once first, too. The first enterprise-facing product, Metropolis, flopped in the market; the second attempt, Foundry, finally opened the books. The turning point was Airbus: in the Toulouse factory, an A380 fuel pump fault kept recurring, and Airbus's own engineers had chased it for two full years with no clue. Palantir's people came in, piped the sensor data into the platform, and cracked the case in two weeks—fuel sloshed away from the pump body during climb. A trivial fix secured an order reportedly worth tens of billions of dollars.

Airbus's head of digital transformation, Mark Fontaine, later said publicly: "The same problem used to take us twenty-four months." (Source: Appendix C.) Airbus went on to become Palantir's most enthusiastic evangelist in Europe, building its entire Skywise data platform on top of it, connecting tens of thousands of aircraft and over fifty thousand users.

### Eruption: The Bootcamp Ignites a Commercial Empire

In 2023, generative AI erupted. Every enterprise wanted to "use AI," and every enterprise got stuck in the same place: they didn't know how. Palantir's answer was to compress twenty years of on-site methodology into an industrial machine—the AIP Bootcamp.

The rules were minimal: the customer brings real data and a real problem, Palantir's Forward Deployed Engineers stand alongside them, and in one to five days they produce a deployable AI application prototype, free or at a token fee. Day 0 locks down an extremely focused core battleground; Day 1 wires up the data and builds the ontology model; Days 2 to 3, engineers and the customer's technical staff write code back-to-back to plug the large language model into the business flow; Days 4 to 5, executives operate the system with their own hands and make decisions on the spot.

The throughput and conversion rate of this machine made the whole industry stare in disbelief: starting from under a hundred pilots in 2022 and doubling year after year, by the 2025 peak it was running nearly 6 sessions a day on average; the enterprise software nine-to-twelve-month sales cycle was compressed to weeks; the pharmacy chain Walgreens deployed four thousand stores in eight months through Bootcamps; clients like the market-research firm J.D. Power, once they learned the method, started running Bootcamps for their own customers—the pattern began to self-replicate. The contracting pace after Bootcamps was so fast that peers might not believe it: a large healthcare company signed a five-year, $26-million-a-year agreement five weeks after attending a Bootcamp; a global bank signed $2 million first after a one-month pilot, then expanded four months later into a three-year, $19-million-a-year contract. (Source: Appendix C.)

The financial results exploded accordingly: in Q4 2025, U.S. commercial revenue grew 137% year over year; the "Rule of 40" hit 127%, and in Q1 2026 reached 145%; it signed $4.26 billion in a single quarter, with Net Revenue Retention of 139%; its market cap at one point broke $400 billion. The Wall Street analysts who had mocked its "human-wave tactic" started rewriting their research reports overnight.

The biggest vote of confidence came from the U.S. Navy. In December 2025, the Secretary of the Navy and Karp jointly announced the "Shipbuilding Operating System" contract: $448 million, using Foundry and AIP to modernize the submarine industrial base—two large shipyards, three naval shipyards, a hundred suppliers. The pilot data was given earlier: scheduling went from 160 hours to 10 minutes, material review from weeks to one hour. The Secretary of the Navy specifically stated "this is not a concept, not a pilot, not a study"—it skipped proof-of-concept and went straight into production, because the earlier embedded pilots had already proven the value. The same year, the U.S. Army bundled 75 scattered services contracts into a single ten-year, $10-billion framework agreement and handed it to Palantir. The government's trust is compound interest accumulated over twenty years of on-site deployment. (Source: Appendix C.)

A more vivid footnote was left by an engineer, Nabeel Qureshi, who spent a full year deployed on-site in Toulouse: four days a week he lived in the final-assembly workshop, writing software for the A350 production line alongside manufacturing workers—he called the thing "an Asana for building airplanes." A Silicon Valley engineer living a full year in a factory in a foreign country—that is the plainest literal meaning of the four words "forward deployed." (Source: Appendix C.)

### Methodology Restored

Looking back at the first seven chapters, the Palantir case is almost an index to the whole book: the demo loop and "customers don't know what they want" (Chapter 2); customization as product discovery, gravel roads and highways (Chapters 1, 7); the strategic value of lighthouse customers (Chapter 3); the portfolio logic of free pilots (Chapter 6); bottom-up on-site tools growing into a platform (Chapter 7); and the most foundational creed—complexity cannot be eliminated remotely, someone has to be on-site (the whole book).

## 8.2 OpenAI: When the People Who Built ChatGPT Start Carrying Bricks

### The Pivot: From "Model as Product" to "Deployment as Strategy"

OpenAI's first seven years were a pure technological epic: the GPT series raced ahead, and ChatGPT set the record for the fastest user growth in human history. In that company narrative, "enterprise delivery" sounded like someone else's business.

The pivot came in 2024. Data from the enterprise market said something that could not be ignored: that "95% failure rate" later widely known was playing out in real time; at the same time, the share map of the enterprise large-model market was shifting violently—industry tracking showed Anthropic climbing to about 32% of the enterprise market, while OpenAI fell from an early absolute lead of about 50% to about 25%. The gap in model capability was narrowing, and the standard by which enterprise customers voted with real money quietly became a different question: **"Who can actually help me get this thing working?"**

OpenAI's response came in two steps. The first step, in 2024, was forming a Forward Deployed Engineering team—starting from 2 engineers under Colin Jarvis, rapidly expanding into a global network spread across San Francisco, New York, London, Dublin, Munich, Paris, Zurich, Tokyo, Singapore, and Sydney, growing from 2 to 52 within a year. The job posting was unblinking: "embed into customers where model performance is mission-critical, delivery is imminent, and ambiguity is the default state," with travel up to 50%. The European head, Fournier, put it more bluntly: "Demand exceeded our expectations." (Source: Appendix C.)

The second step, on May 11, 2026, was the formation of the "Deployment Company"—a joint venture controlled by OpenAI, joined by 19 top capital providers (TPG leading, Advent, Bain Capital, and Brookfield co-leading, Goldman Sachs, SoftBank, Warburg Pincus participating, with Bain Consulting, Capgemini, and McKinsey as founding partners), with an initial investment of over $4 billion and a reported pre-money valuation of about $10 billion; simultaneously acquiring the applied-AI consulting firm Tomoro, with roughly 150 deployment engineers folded in as a unit. Chief Operating Officer Brad Lightcap took personal command.

The structural design of this new company belongs in a business-school textbook, because it welded "where the customers come from" and "how the capital returns" together. The funders are private equity giants, and these giants hold hundreds of portfolio companies spanning healthcare, manufacturing, finance, retail, and logistics—they are the Deployment Company's ready-made customer pool; in exchange, OpenAI reportedly promised these private equity funders a 17.5% annualized minimum return over five years (media report, not confirmed by OpenAI), while retaining super-voting rights and control of strategic direction.

The acquired Tomoro was no nobody either: this British applied-AI consulting firm's engineers had previously done exactly enterprise deployment work at Tesco, Virgin Atlantic, and the games company Supercell. In a single day, OpenAI assembled three cards—"engineers, channel, capital." (Source: Appendix C.)

A company valued in the hundreds of billions of dollars, holding humanity's most powerful models, setting up a separate ten-billion-dollar company just to "send people to work on-site at the customer"—this is the heaviest counterweight in the history of the FDE pattern.

### On the Ground: John Deere's Farmland

OpenAI's FDE methodology's best showcase is its partnership with John Deere. This nearly two-hundred-year-old agricultural giant needed to solve a problem that grows out of the soil: weed control. The traditional approach is to spray herbicide across the whole field—high cost, heavy crop damage, big environmental toll. The ideal of precision agriculture is "spray only where the weeds are," but the difficulty of identification lies in: every field has a different weed profile, different crop growth, different season.

The OpenAI forward deployment team's approach was a complete demonstration of the methodology in Chapters 2 and 4: fly to Iowa, follow the agronomy experts into the fields, understand the precision-agronomy workflow and its constraints (including the non-negotiable deadline—the farming season); review hundreds of real field-operation cases alongside the experts, coding "what counts as a good spraying recommendation" into a custom evaluation system; then rapidly iterate the model and system under the supervision of that evaluation system. The final result: chemical usage reduced by up to 70%, and farmers' interaction frequency rose 6x.

This case hides three details worth underlining. First, the deadline is the farming season, not the project schedule—the FDE rhythm must obey the customer's business cadence. Second, the evaluation system precedes model optimization—first define "good," then pursue "good." Third, the 70% number is not post-hoc PR packaging; it was a target set jointly with the experts before work began.

### On the Ground: BBVA's One Hundred Twenty Thousand People

Another benchmark is BBVA. The starting point was unglamorous—deploying enterprise ChatGPT. But the compound interest of the FDE pattern is: the starting point is a tool, the path is the organization. Starting from 3,300 accounts, employees spontaneously created over 20,000 custom mini-assistants, 83% of people used them actively every week, saving about 3 hours per week on average; a year and a half later, the deployment had expanded into a system covering 25 countries and 120,000 employees, and the bank's goal had upgraded accordingly to "building an AI-native global bank." From "give a tool" to "co-build a form of organization"—this is the extreme shape of Chapter 6's "deepening the installed base." (Source: Appendix C.)

### Methodology Restored

The lesson of the OpenAI case lies in the "self-awareness of a model company": as model capabilities converge, deployment capability becomes the main battlefield of differentiation; as the product form remains unset (no one knows what an agent should look like), the field becomes the only site of product discovery. Its three-phase working method (co-creation, validation, delivery), its engineering obsession with evaluation systems, and the capital-structure innovation of the Deployment Company (using private equity's industrial networks as customer channels) correspond respectively to the core propositions of Chapters 4, 6, and 3 of this book.

## 8.3 Anthropic and the Vertical Three: Three Variations of FDE

If Palantir invented the pattern and OpenAI pushed it onto the front page, then Anthropic and a batch of vertical-AI companies show three variations of FDE in different soils.

### Variation One: Anthropic—The Safety Gene and "Auditable" Differentiation

Anthropic's FDE sits under the Applied AI team, and its job posting carries two qualifiers worth savoring: one is "safe and reliable"—all delivery must meet Anthropic's safety standards; the other is "defending the company's mission on the front line"—FDE is simultaneously an ambassador of values. In 2025, this team expanded at fivefold-in-a-year speed; its lead, Kate Dejong, explained: "The needs of a Fortune 500 bank and an AI-native startup are entirely different species." (Source: Appendix C.)

The direct product of that differentiation is that "auditability" became a product feature. The partnership with the fintech giant FIS is the benchmark: Anthropic's Forward Deployed Engineers embedded inside FIS to jointly design financial-crime-detection agents, compressing anti-money-laundering investigations from hours to minutes, with every decision replayable and traceable end to end, first landing at Bank of Montreal and Amalgamated Bank. In heavily regulated industries, "every AI decision can be explained clearly to a regulator" is not a nice-to-have, it's the admission ticket—Anthropic made it a selling point.

The evidence that "security paranoia" became the enterprise-market killer weapon is written in customers' reviews. The cybersecurity firm Palo Alto Networks deployed Claude to 2,500 developers; an engineering director's verdict was direct: "They care more about security than anyone else—every meeting talks about the security impact. As the largest cybersecurity company, that matters to us." The result: feature-development speed up twenty to thirty percent, junior developers completing integration tasks 70% faster. The pharma giant Novo Nordisk uses it to write clinical research documents: reports that used to take weeks now go out in ten minutes. At the same time, in partnership with the consulting giant Accenture, it trained thirty thousand Claude-specialist consultants, and its enterprise customer count went from under a thousand to over 300,000 in two years. (Source: Appendix C.)

A deeper layer of difference lies in the official commitment to "knowledge transfer": the stated goal of embedding is for FIS to "build and scale its own agents going forward." Writing "teach the customer" into the contract short-term looks like weakening your own irreplaceability, but long-term is the strongest answer to "vendor lock-in" doubts—Gartner predicts that before 2028, 70% of enterprises will abandon such solutions due to vendor cost and skill hollowing; Anthropic's approach is defusing this time bomb in advance.

In May 2026, Anthropic was reported to be forming an enterprise-AI services joint venture with Blackstone and others, reportedly about $1.5 billion in scale, focused on embedding Claude into mid-market enterprise operations—squaring off against OpenAI's Deployment Company on the same day. Two model giants completing the organization of "deployment as strategy" on the very same day—this fact itself, more than any analysis, tells you where FDE sits on the map.

### Variation Two: Sierra and Decagon—The Startup's "Delivery as Product"

Sierra (founded in 2023 by former Salesforce co-CEO Bret Taylor and former Google VP Clay Bavor, valued at $4.5 billion at its October 2024 funding round, reportedly climbing further since) turned the FDE pattern into the business model itself: customers don't pay for software, they pay for "resolved sessions"; implementation is handled by Sierra's forward deployment team, and the customer only needs to contribute brand voice and operating rules. Results: Sonos, Casper, WeightWatchers, ADT and other brand customers flocked in; the fastest publicly reported go-live case was four weeks, and annual contract thresholds generally started in the hundreds of thousands of dollars. Outcome-based pricing anchors Sierra's pricing naturally to customer value—the most aggressive real-world sample of Chapter 6's "outcome-based pricing."

Sierra's internal name for the role is also worth noting: not FDE, but "Agent Engineer." Its lead, Natalie Meurer (five years at Palantir, working law-enforcement, defense, and infrastructure clients), explained: the name should describe "the shape of the technical work," not just "obsession with the customer"—building a voice AI agent requires a special kind of "taste" (what sounds like a human, what sounds right), a craft narrower and deeper than generic FDE. The ticketing platform Vivid Seats partnership is her team's brightest battle report: four-week go-live, self-service resolution rate up 40%, customer satisfaction up 35%—and the mechanism by which conversation data feeds back into the product lets "ten thousand people asking for the same feature in a month" jump straight onto the priority list. (Source: Appendix C.)

Decagon (co-founder Ashwin Sreenivas out of Palantir, total funding $231 million) took a different path: it productized its delivery experience directly into agent operating procedures—letting the customer's operations staff define multi-step workflows in natural language, with the AI executing deterministically. Customer list: Notion, Duolingo, Eventbrite, Rippling. Reportedly, Chime's self-service resolution rate hit 70%, Duolingo's issue-triage rate 80%, ClassPass's customer-service cost dropped 95%. Decagon's FDE plays more of a role of "the person who teaches the customer's team to use this system"—the goal of delivery is to give the customer's non-technical people self-service capability, the best practice of "can it be used by people who don't write code," one of the "four questions of productization" in Chapter 7. (Source: Appendix C.)

### Variation Three: Harvey—Breaking Into Places Ordinary Software Can't Reach

The legal industry is the "Bermuda Triangle" of enterprise software: top law firms have strict confidentiality discipline, data cannot leave the domain, partners each rule their own roost—self-service online software rarely wins a single battle here. Harvey (founded in 2022, valued at $11 billion after a March 2026 funding round) forced its way in with the FDE pattern.

First, remember this company's pedigree: the two founders—one a litigation lawyer out of a law firm, the other a scientist out of DeepMind (Google's AI lab); a $5 million seed round, from the OpenAI Startup Fund. (Source: Appendix C.)

Replaying its playbook reads almost like a perfect string of this book's methodology. Lighthouse strategy—the first big client locked in the global top-tier law firm Allen & Overy (February 2023, 3,500 lawyers, 43 offices), followed by PwC (4,000+ legal professionals), Freshfields, and Macfarlanes; champion management—Allen & Overy's internal point of contact, David Wakeling (head of market innovation), became the in-firm axis of the deployment; rhythm of penetration—cut in with a single business group, driven by partner champions, expand horizontally after six months of live combat; customer-research loop—partners sign the check but don't do the work, paralegals do the work but don't sign the check, knowledge-management lawyers see the blind spots of both sides, and the FDE interview system must pierce all three layers at once, "the hardest part of the deployment isn't the model, it's the customer-research loop"; ultra-long cycle—six to nine months per firm deployment, with FDE responsible for everything from the data pipeline out of the document-management system all the way to per-partner adoption demos.

The results are a surge rarely seen in vertical AI: over 100,000 lawyer users, 1,300 organizations, covering most of the American top-100 law firms; annual recurring revenue grew from about $100 million in August 2025 to about $190 million in January 2026; valuation quadruple-jumped in a single year—$3 billion, $5 billion, $8 billion, $11 billion. (Source: Appendix C.)

Harvey proves where the boundary of the FDE pattern lies: **wherever "confidentiality regimes, data residency, and key-user autonomy" all three mountains stand at once, productized delivery has no answer; only by sending people in can you bring the system in.** And such markets—law, healthcare, finance, government—are precisely the highest-margin enterprise-service markets on earth.

## 8.4 The China Cases: Growing FDE on "Project-Based" Soil

China is the world's most unusual enterprise software market: it has the hardest-working delivery engineers, the most exacting customization demands, and the deepest "project-based curse"—big enterprises want customization, vendors lose money on every deal, costs can't be amortized, everyone degenerates into the client's outsourcing shop, specialization hollows out. The fate of the FDE pattern on this soil is a question that must be answered on its own: is it the antidote to the curse, or the curse's new mask?

First see how tangled this trap is, and listen to three practitioners' sighs. The first comes from the database company DolphinDB; an industry long-form asked "Who Is Destroying China's Enterprise Software Industry," and the answer was six mountains: freeloading, open source, outsourcing, bidding, data-science-services, and a deformed market structure—China's top engineering talent, long consumed in "highly customized, relationship-driven, price-war" non-standard delivery. The second comes from a veteran of enterprise software on the podcast *Hardground Hacker*: "Customization is the natural enemy of SaaS, and this curse can only dissolve on its own the day the market matures." The third comes from the former president of DingTalk: many companies get stuck at one to two hundred million in revenue—customer acquisition is hard, delivery is hard, operations are hard, customization cost is high, and economies of scale are simply out of reach. 36Kr summarized these three sighs: "Software productization amortizes the R&D cost of project-based work; SaaS amortizes the sales cost—only project-based companies suffer most, because project cost becomes invalid the moment it leaves the room, with no way to amortize it." (Source: Appendix C.) These three sighs are exactly the China question the FDE pattern must answer.

### The Big-Tech Path: Volcano Engine's Doubao FDE

Volcano Engine, under ByteDance, is the Chinese internet giant that has most explicitly flown the FDE flag: in 2026 it set up a dedicated FDE team to go deep into industries and co-create with benchmark customers. Volcano Engine president Tan Xingyu's public-interview phrasing almost word-for-word echoes this book's definition: "FDE is not sales, and not pre-sales; they must have strong technical delivery capability, especially the ability to deliver AI code." He also disclosed that team members are deliberately staffed with diverse industry backgrounds (for instance, putting someone from a bioengineering background onto the biopharma industry), and they now cover automotive, healthcare, education, finance, semiconductors, and other key industries. In July 2026, Volcano Engine signed a strategic partnership with the consulting giant EY, with the two sides planning to build a thousand-person FDE team to "explore a new model of full-stack AI delivery." (Source: Appendix C.)

The big-tech player's built-in advantage in doing FDE is a thick platform base (Volcano Ark provides an integrated base of models, toolchain, and cloud infrastructure); the built-in challenge is the big-tech sales-system inertia and the financial inertia that "delivery is a cost center"—whether the FDE team can attain Palantir-style organizational status of "the field is R&D" determines whether it is real FDE or a pre-sales support department under a new name.

### The Pioneer Path: Writing "FDE ≠ On-Site Outsourcing" into the Website

More worth watching is a batch of local startups. They don't have the big-tech platform base, yet they use the clearest possible language to distinguish FDE from the old species of the Chinese market. A service provider focused on the real-estate and facilities-management industry wrote on its website what is currently the most refined defining distinction of FDE in China:

> One, FDE delivers by phase and accepts by result, while on-site outsourcing bills by the hour; two, FDE brings a platform base to engineer on top of, while on-site outsourcing writes from scratch on the spot; three, FDE finishes and leaves, with capability settled in the system and your team, while on-site outsourcing stays longer and longer, and when the person leaves the system stops.

Three sentences, respectively corresponding to three core propositions of this book: outcome-based pricing (Chapter 6), platform base (Chapter 7), and knowledge transfer (Chapter 5). It also built a "discouragement mechanism"—don't take work whose scenarios haven't been validated, don't take work whose data can be exported in one table, don't take work from people who just want to "learn about AI," "we'd rather you start a little later than have you start at the wrong time." This is the conscious practice in the China market of Chapter 2's "refuse the expensive POC purgatory."

Another service provider moved the FDE pattern into the most compliance-sensitive part of finance, and distilled an explicit three-phase methodology: scenario diagnosis one to two weeks (identify the three to five core scenarios with the highest value, quantify ROI) → embedded delivery eight to sixteen weeks (full-stack delivery from model selection to production integration) → production deployment and continuous evolution (used daily by the business, iterating with the business), while emphasizing "data never leaves the domain"—an on-the-ground adaptation to the regulatory environment of Chinese financial institutions. (Source: Appendix C.)

In June 2026, a practitioner at a Chinese investment firm posted a long essay online with a pointed title: *The Hottest Job in Silicon Valley This Year, FDE—We've Been Burying Our Heads in It for Three Years*. The piece records the felt experience of putting this playbook to work in Chinese enterprise services: forward deployed engineers "dive into a single customer after signing, with no cap on capability, and actually fit AI into their business"; the measure of success "isn't how many lines of code were written or how many documents were delivered, but whether anyone is actually using this system and whether the customer's business has gotten better"; and that summary that is in perfect agreement with this book—"go-live isn't the end; he has to stay until this system becomes the customer's daily routine and the customer's own people can take over." This article was forwarded heavily in domestic practitioner circles, because for the first time it laid out in Chinese a correspondence table: the product engineer owns the roadmap, pre-sales owns the deal, implementation owns the acceptance form, customer success owns the renewal—and FDE owns "whether the customer's conversion and productivity actually rose." (Source: Appendix C.)

### The Control Group: Set-Top Boxes and Paper Contracts

Whom should FDE invest in, and whom not? Wu Guangyu, CEO of Baidao Data, gave two sharply contrasting clients at a founders' roundtable.

One was a set-top-box client with an extremely clear need—"how to use AI inside a set-top box." FDE soaked with them and ended up building an agent that watches TV with you and gossips about it, with a very high input-output ratio. The other was an overseas client whose boss opened by wanting to invest a million dollars in AI, yet at every contract signing would print the contract out and circle-and-mark it with a pen, not even using Word's track changes. Wu Guangyu's judgment was blunt: "A client with this little digital foundation—putting FDE in will definitely produce nothing. We told him straight: first buy a hundred Gemini accounts and get everyone using them, then talk once you have concrete ideas."

His conclusion is cut from the same cloth as Chapter 2's refusal mechanism: **the prerequisite of FDE is that the customer has a clear point of need.** Don't easily invest in fuzzy, boundary-less demands; you can break them into phase one, phase two, phase three, do the clear part first, and once you've delivered results, the follow-on needs can naturally convert into a long-term services contract. (Source: Appendix C.)

### The Other Side: The Vacuum Layer and the Dirty Work

But if you think Chinese FDE practice is all the clean stuff above, front-line practitioners will hand you a cup of cold water.

The *Growth Hacker AI Weekly* recorded the observations of front-line executor Shen Yue: in quite a few domestic projects, FDE is more like vendor-side outsourced labor, doing the dirty, thankless work of filling pits and wiping backsides. In a ten-million-yuan state-owned-enterprise project she personally experienced, the big boss signed the contract and then there was no follow-up; there was no one genuinely in charge on the project, and FDE was hung in a vacuum layer—no decision-maker above, no executor below, so in the end they could only fight for acceptance by studying "reporting logic." Another private home-decoration company in Guangdong: the second-generation factory boss opened with: help me cut 6,000 people down to 3,000. But AI can replace tasks, not positions; the project ground forward amid repeated scoldings.

Shen Yue's summary is worth copying here: the hard part of Chinese FDE lies in the layers between the demand side, in enterprises' weak digital foundation, in bosses' lack of patience—and the over-promises the front-end sales makes to sell the product all end up on FDE's tab. She has another line, which I quoted as the epigraph of Chapter 4: "No matter how technology evolves, the human nature and power-responsibility games inside an organization are always a harder problem than the technology." (Source: Appendix C.)

### Another Voice: Is FDE Actually a Good Business?

The doubts don't come only from the execution layer. At the same roundtable, Wu Xiankun, founder of Kuse.ai, raised doubts about the FDE pattern itself, in no light terms: "FDE is very hard to make into a good business model." His reasoning was direct: done shallowly, the model vendors will do it themselves; done deeply, it's still essentially outsourcing, with high delivery cost and hard to scale. "Serving state-owned and central enterprises, you can't refuse out-of-scope demands—where is your leverage? Will you doubt, in the dead of night, that you've become cheap outsourcing—this is a question everyone doing FDE has to face."

His solution was fairly radical: AI Roll-up (consolidation through acquisition). Since customers worry about data leaks and teams feel outsourcing betrays the original intent, then buy the company outright, make everyone a shareholder, align interests, and flip the whole company inside out; once remade, keep acquiring the next one with the same methodology. His logic: if AI transformation really has that much value, then you should own the business, not sell person-hours. (Source: Appendix C.)

This doubt may not have a standard answer, but it and the Gartner prediction cited in Chapter 5—that before 2028, 70% of enterprises may abandon FDE-led solutions—form a strange echo: **the biggest enemy of the FDE pattern may not be technology, but its own cost structure.** This book does not intend to give a standard answer to it, but it is a question every person thinking of entering should work out first.

### The Three Special Propositions of Chinese FDE

Synthesizing these practices and industry observations, for FDE to hold in China, it must answer three questions its American counterparts don't have to answer.

First, the pricing dilemma. The U.S. market can accept subscription, usage-based billing, even outcome-sharing; the muscle memory of Chinese customers is "buyout plus project-based acceptance." A viable transitional form is a "hybrid": payment by phase acceptance (going with the habit) + value metrics written into the acceptance clause (injecting the outcome gene) + annual operations-and-evolution contract (cultivating renewal consciousness). Copying per-resolution billing in one step will die in the procurement office in most industries.

Second, the platform dilemma. The economics of FDE rely on "platform base plus on-site customization," while the problem of a large swath of Chinese software companies is exactly "they have the field but no platform"—every deal is a headcount business writing code from zero. For these companies, what matters more than copying the FDE form is backfilling FDE's foundation: first settle the highest-frequency customization into reusable components, then talk about forward deployment. Without the replication leverage of Chapter 7, FDE in China will only degenerate into "nicely named on-site outsourcing"—which is exactly why the local pioneers wrote "bring a platform base" into the definition.

Third, the talent dilemma and the talent opportunity. China's engineer culture—able to endure hardship, fast to respond, full-stack, used to solving everything on the customer site—is precisely closest to what FDE demands, which is the "opportunity"; but the "dilemma" is that over the past twenty years, such talent has been trapped in the person-day-billed outsourcing system, and the market's price signal has long undervalued them. The popularization of the FDE concept in China may bring a profound side effect: **re-pricing hundreds of thousands of China's delivery engineers.** When they learn that their counterparts across the ocean pull a median $385,000 annual salary, this industry's value coordinates will loosen.

The established enterprise-software companies are corroborating this judgment too. SAP's president for Greater China, Yuan Xin, said on a podcast that the forward deployment engineer and the traditional ERP consultant's roles are fusing, and the scarcest thing right now is "composite talent that both has product-engineering capability and deeply understands the business." She also offered a line worth remembering: the biggest challenge in AI going live is organizational inertia, not technology—the historical debt of foundational data architecture won't vanish on its own just because AI showed up. (Source: Appendix C.)

### Judgment

FDE will not cure all the chronic ills of Chinese enterprise software, but it offers a rare moment of "vindication": redefining "being close to the customer" from a marker of low-end outsourcing into a marker of high-end capability. What the Chinese market lacks is not engineers willing to go on-site, but the platforms, methodology, and pricing structures that let on-site work produce compound interest. Whoever first backfills these three pieces will, in this world's largest and most complex enterprise-service market, grow China's Palantir—or, grow some new species not yet named.

## 8.5 180 Days of an AI Startup: A Full FDE Field Replay

The last case comes from a Chinese AI startup I tracked through field research (anonymized at the team's request, hereafter "Company N"). It has neither Palantir's platform nor OpenAI's halo, but its 180-day journey fully demonstrates how this book's methodology operates on a team with limited resources. To protect commercial information, the numbers have been blurred, but the proportions and decision logic are kept real.

### Days 1 to 30: Pick the Battleground, Don't Chase the Contract

Company N builds enterprise knowledge-base AI and has three prospective customer leads: a top-tier brokerage (big budget, grand ask), a regional retail chain (medium budget, concrete pain point), and a Grade-A tertiary hospital (big influence, sensitive data). The sales instinct is to lunge at the brokerage. But the team, following the methods of Chapters 2 and 3, ran a triple check and entry diligence: the brokerage's problem was a "cosmic-grade ask" (the first meeting wanted to cover the whole company), with no clear business owner; the hospital's pain point was real, but the data-residency restriction made the delivery cycle uncontrollable; the retail chain's CFO was personally minding the project, the pain point was concrete ("the operations-supervision reports for 3,000 stores—regional managers simply can't read them all"), and the data foundation, though messy, was reachable.

The team chose the retail chain—lighthouse value not the highest, but the triple check passed in full. This is the discipline of section 3.1: **schedule by strategic value and learning value, not by contract size.**

### Days 31 to 75: Entry, the Shadow Work Method, and a "Big Plan That Got Cut"

Two engineers and one industry consultant entered the site. For the first two weeks not a single line of production code was written; what they did was the shadow work method: follow the regional managers on store walks, watch how they chase store data in WeChat groups and build weekly reports in spreadsheets. The key discovery came from a "workaround": regional managers didn't look at the reports in the company's data system at all; the data source they actually trusted was a form the store managers hand-filled every day—because "the system's data is three days late, and it's wrong all the time."

This discovery overturned the original big plan (an intelligent data-analysis platform), and the team narrowed the Minimum Viable Deployment down to one small cut: **build only one "store anomaly daily report"**—every morning at eight, aggregate the previous day's anomaly signals across 3,000 stores (sales swings, inventory anomalies, complaint spikes) into a daily report readable in under three minutes, and push it to the regional managers' WeChat. Real data, single pain point, six-week deadline.

### Days 76 to 120: The Hidden War of Activation

Going live was only the beginning. The activation phase ran into two textbook obstacles. The first was trust calibration: in the first week, the AI misjudged two stores' promotion peaks as "anomalous spikes," and the managers griped in the group. The team didn't defend itself; within 48 hours it wired the "promotion calendar" into the judgment logic, and—the key move—publicly thanked the manager who had reported the error, in the group. The second was champion management: a senior regional director (the uncrowned-king type) initially watched coldly from the sidelines; the team invited him to participate in revising the evaluation criteria, and his three pieces of experience were written into the system's rules. Two weeks later, he had become the system's most zealous evangelist.

On Day 90, the natural open rate of the daily report stabilized above 85%. On Day 120, the retail group's CIO proactively showcased the system at the quarterly operating review—the internal champion had been armed into a salesperson.

### Days 121 to 180: The First Brick of Replication

The renewal negotiation was without suspense, but the team took two more important actions. First, it distilled the assets used over and over in this delivery: the retail-industry data-ingestion component, the "anomaly signal" evaluation framework, the store-scenario due-diligence checklist—the first scenario playbook took shape. Second, it used the retail chain's case (the customer agreed to a joint release) to knock on the door of a second customer—a fast-moving-consumer-goods brand's channel-management department, with an isomorphic scenario. The second project's delivery cycle was 40% shorter than the first's: the replication flywheel had turned its first lap.

180 days, a small team, no funding news, no disruptive technology. But it validates the plainest conclusion of the whole book: **the right problem + real data + close-in service + the discipline of sediment = a snowball that can roll.**

Five sets of cases, done. From intelligence agencies to Iowa farmland, from top-tier law firms to Chinese retail chains—the wider the span of the scenes, the clearer the commonality of the methodology. Two questions remain: what boundaries should such a force hold itself to (the afterword); and the entrant's action checklist (the appendix).
