## 3.1 Selecting Your Lighthouse Customer

The first lesson of internet product customer acquisition is selecting seed users: 100 users who love you beat 10,000 who think you're okay. The FDE counterpart is the lighthouse customer—the kind that doesn't just give you revenue, but sends a signal across an entire industry.

The strategic value of a lighthouse is multiplied several times over under the FDE model, for three reasons.

First, the lighthouse is the strongest sales asset. Enterprise customers have long decision chains and high risk aversion; peer endorsement is the shortest persuasion path. Harvey's origin story is a textbook case: the first major customer announced in February 2023 was Linklaters, a top global law firm with 3,500 lawyers and 43 offices. Once that lighthouse lit up, PwC, Cleary Gottlieb, and other big clients followed—law is an industry that cares deeply about pedigree, and landing Linklaters meant a passport to the entire top-tier law firm market. Palantir's early history rhymes: the CIA was its most demanding and most endorsement-worthy customer, and the intelligence community's trust later became the key that unlocked Wall Street, manufacturing, and government markets.

The process by which Harvey lit that lighthouse is itself an FDE lesson. In November 2022, Linklaters set up a dedicated team—the Market Innovation Group, led by partner David Wakeling—to begin secretly piloting the product of a then-unknown startup. Note how they validated: not by watching demos, but through firm-wide real combat. By the end of the pilot, 3,500 lawyers had posed roughly 40,000 real work questions to the system, covering 250 practice areas and 50 languages. Wakeling's verdict was the line quoted repeatedly across the legal world: "I've been in legal tech for 15 years, and I've never seen anything that changes the game like this." Another partner showed a reporter a concrete example: asking the system to prepare a ten-page brief on "how to open a bank in Luxembourg" for an American client—"It actually produced it." (Source: Appendix C.)

Second, the lighthouse determines your product DNA. Under the FDE model, field learning flows back into the product—which means your first ten customers de facto help shape your product. Pick the wrong lighthouse, and the product gets pulled toward a direction with no generality. a16z's first piece of advice to startups—"sell smartly"—speaks to exactly this: a young company can't satisfy everyone, so pick an ideal customer profile that has at least some commonality in system environment and use case, so that the learning from each delivery accumulates instead of canceling out.

Third, lighthouse quality matters an order of magnitude more than quantity. Your delivery capacity is inherently scarce—an elite team can deeply serve only a single-digit number of customers at once; taking on the wrong one costs you not "one less deal," but half a year of a top team mired in a swamp. So the three high-risk signals from Chapter 2 (no business owner, refusal to provide real data, cosmic-scale demands) must be checked here too, plus one test unique to lighthouses: negotiate early in the engagement whether, after success, they're willing to step forward—co-publishing a case study, speaking at industry conferences, hosting visits from your potential customers. A lighthouse unwilling to speak up publicly for you is worth at least half as much.

### Beware the "Requirement Locusts"

Building internet products means watching out for "product locusts"—early users who swarm in, use and leave, and mislead the product direction. The FDE counterpart is the "requirement locust": a customer with ample budget and voracious demand, but who drains your team without producing any compound returns. Three identifying traits: the requirements clearly diverge from the company's strategic direction, so even if delivered, no reusable capability accrues; they treat FDE as cheap outsourcing, assigning work by headcount rather than aligning by results; and internal politics consume enormous energy, so your main job becomes helping one department prove another department wrong. Saying no to requirement locusts is hard—their contract values are often tempting. But remember Barry's ledger: a wrong pilot burns away not just current costs, but the team's time, morale, and the product compounding that could have grown on the right customer.

## 3.2 Start with the Dumbest Things

The famed incubator YC has an old maxim: "Do things that don't scale." The earliest founders of the home-rental platform went door to door photographing landlords' listings; the founders of Stripe, the online payments company, installed software for users on the spot.

When asked on a podcast about the relationship between FDE and this maxim, McGrew gave a precise formulation: **the FDE model is doing unscalable things at scale**.

This sentence lays bare the philosophical undertone of FDE customer acquisition: in the enterprise market, there is no scalable shortcut to earning trust—only dumb methods. Three layers, each deepening the last.

The first layer: people must show up. On a16z's advice list for forward deployment teams, the last item is just four words: show up in person. The reasoning is "a cliché, but clichés become clichés because they're true"—showing up not only improves sales, it multiplies the success rate when mapping the customer's internal power dynamics and driving adoption of new tools. Enterprise trust is priced in "number of meetings" and "shared experiences." Video calls build familiarity; only fighting side by side builds trust.

The second layer: get your hands dirty. OpenAI's FDEs followed agronomists into the fields of Iowa; Palantir's engineers lived for weeks on oil rig drilling platforms and aircraft final assembly lines; Harvey's FDEs ran adoption demos partner by partner inside law firms. None of these scenes was "efficient," but each one produced something remote communication could never produce: a firsthand feel for the customer's situation. That bodily feel translates directly into solution quality—only after you've stepped through the mud of a farm field do you understand why that seemingly perfect phone interface is completely unusable under outdoor sunlight.

The third layer: be a servant first, a mentor later. The most common mistake when FDEs first enter a site is arriving with a "we're here to save you" posture. Get the posture wrong, and every intelligence channel shuts down. The right sequence is to first do the most unglamorous services: fix a data issue for the customer's analyst, fill in an API doc for the IT department, automate a weekly report for a business team. These "dumb tasks" buy three strategic assets: a real map of the organization (who actually decides, who is trusted, who is the hidden key node), the truth of the data environment (which dataset is trusted, which is window dressing), and most importantly—the credential of "this outsider is one of us." Once that identity is established, what you say starts to carry weight.

The end of dumb methods is not staying dumb forever. Chapter 7 covers how to distill these dumb efforts into repeatable playbooks—but before you can scale, you must first wade through the mud to carve out the path worth copying.

## 3.3 The Trust Dividend: The Endorsement Vein in Benchmark Customers

Enterprise procurement is a market of "extreme information asymmetry, extreme cost of failure," where decision-makers rank their trust in information on a steep gradient: the vendor's own marketing is less trusted than analyst reports; analyst reports, less than peers' public case studies; peers' public case studies, less than peers' private recommendations. That last tier—"someone I know used it and said it actually works"—converts at a rate that crushes every other channel. And the FDE model is precisely the best machine for producing "raw material for private recommendations": what you deliver is not a software license, it's the line a customer executive drops at a peer dinner—"they really know what they're doing."

Mining this vein requires action at three layers.

The first layer: make the delivery into "a story worth telling." The precondition for a customer executive to spread the word for you is that your delivery can be told as a story that makes them look good in front of peers. This requires the delivery outcome to have three narrative elements: a specific number ("70% reduction in chemical usage," "anti-money-laundering investigations from hours to minutes"), a specific person ("our agronomist worked in the field with their team"), and a specific contrast ("what used to take three months, this time took five days"). When the delivery wraps up, proactively prepare this narrative for the customer's internal champion—a one-pager, three charts, a 30-second version they can tell. How detailed you make the materials determines whether the retelling stays faithful or drifts.

The second layer: turn the customer's success into the customer's social currency. After Walgreens deployed across four thousand stores in eight months via the bootcamp model, it became a star at industry conferences; the market research firm J.D. Power went further—as a customer, after attending Palantir's bootcamp, it started running bootcamps for its own customers. (Source: Appendix C.) This is the ideal state: the customer presents your methodology as its own industry leadership. The endorsement upgrades from "thank you" to "proud to use you."

The third layer: beware the "backlash mechanism" of endorsements. The enterprise market holds grudges. A failed delivery at a high-profile customer spreads far faster than ten successes—because failure stories go down better with a drink. This is why Chapter 2's "rejection mechanism" and Chapter 4's "activation discipline" matter so much: the Achilles' heel of lighthouse strategy is not failing to find a lighthouse, but letting the lighthouse go dark on your watch.

## 3.4 Mapping the Customer's Books with Data

Before and during FDE entry, there is a move called "entry due diligence": before writing the first line of code, use a structured method to map the customer's entire household. A complete due diligence checklist contains five maps.

- **Data map:** What data sources the customer has, who owns each, what the quality is, who controls access, and what dark data you'd never think of—some veteran employee's stashed spreadsheet, a report that only circulates in email, a paper ledger. The point is not "what exists," but "which is trusted." In almost every organization, the official data source and the data source employees actually trust are not the same; the latter is the one you must tap.

- **Process map:** The real operating picture of the target business process—not the version in the process document, but the version observed through the shadow work method, including every link, exception, and workaround that was never written down. Mark three points in particular: the link that consumes the most time, the link where errors are costliest, and the link where emotions run hottest. These three points are usually where value is richest.

- **Organization map:** Who initiates, who pays, who uses, who can veto, who is the uncrowned king. The most common cause of death in enterprise projects is not technology but a misread organizational account—your internal champion is either not senior enough, or too senior (so senior they have no time for you), or the right people were never brought in. Pay special attention to finding the "knowledge hub": someone not highly titled, but whom everyone goes to with real problems. They are both the best source of requirements and the seed node for later rollout.

- **System map:** The true landscape of the technical environment—the list of systems to integrate and their interface status, security and compliance boundaries, and the change management process. The system map determines your deployment architecture and schedule. Many projects' timelines are doomed to collapse on day one, because nobody asked: releasing a version on the customer's side takes six weeks of process.

- **Political map:** The most sensitive, and the most critical. Whose cheese does this project move? Is the target process for automation a source of some department's power? Whose work will your solution make redundant? That report listed "employees' reluctance to adopt new tools" as the number one roadblock, and the root of resistance is mostly not laziness but fear—fear of being replaced, of being proven incompetent, of losing one's reason to exist. The political map's function is to identify the carriers of these fears in advance and to design "new paths forward" for them in the solution, not "dead ends." Chapter 5's renewal topic returns to this point: the person you design as a "victim" will become the most steadfast opponent at renewal.

Once the five maps are complete, you have truly "entered the site." This due diligence typically takes one to two weeks, with Echo and Delta conducting their pieces separately and syncing daily. It looks like pure cost, but do the math this way: two weeks of due diligence saves you three months of sprinting on the wrong battlefield.

## 3.5 Technical Content Marketing: Building a Trust Engine That Keeps Outputting

Content marketing is a classic weapon for internet companies to acquire customers. The FDE company's acquisition logic dictates that its content marketing has a unique positioning: **not chasing traffic, but chasing "pre-sale of professional trust."**

How enterprise customers find you is not by seeing your ad, but by: hitting a hard problem, searching, asking around, discovering that a company has published an extremely professional take on the issue, and concluding—"they get it, go to them." The role content plays here is "pre-qualification" at the very front of the customer's decision chain. Palantir publishes scenario-based technical articles year-round; OpenAI and Anthropic turn enterprise customer cases into detailed technical narratives; a single landmark essay from a16z set the tone for an entire category—none of these is brand promotion; they are deliberately managed trust assets.

FDE companies' content marketing has three military rules that distinguish it from conventional corporate content.

- **Rule One: Write from the "trench view," not the "booth view."** Conventional corporate content talks about how powerful the product is and how grand the vision; FDE content talks about how hard the problem was and how we slogged through it. Why did that article by Barry, the former Palantir engineer, about forward deployment engineering go viral in the industry? Because it wrote about everything you'd never see from a booth: the waste of reinventing the wheel, the negative-margin pilots, the burned-out engineers—and the result was that this "self-exposure of dirty laundry" became the best evangelism for the Palantir model. Truth from the trenches carries natural penetrating power, because readers can tell: who is speaking marketing talking points, and who is speaking the reality they themselves live in every day.

- **Rule Two: Open-source your methodology to earn "the right to be cited."** Publishing your methodology for how you do discovery, how you do validation, how you do delivery—short-term looks like teaching competitors, long-term is defining industry standards. When customers across the industry start using your framework to ask questions ("How is your evaluation system built?" "What does your deployment checklist look like?"), you've gone from vendor to question-setter. In 2026, a practitioner open-source community called OpenFDE emerged and quickly aggregated momentum, proving that this industry's knowledge hunger is far from satisfied; whoever systematically satisfies it first holds the power to define. The writing of this book is, in a sense, a practice of the same logic.

- **Rule Three: Make the customer's internal champion the hero of the content.** The byline logic of a case article is subtle: the protagonist should be the visionary executive on the customer side, and your team is "the partner who helped them succeed." Giving the champion a stage is the same as handing an invitation to "become him" to the same kind of role in the next potential customer.

## 3.6 Getting Past Procurement, Legal, and Security Review

The "gates" of the enterprise market are the procurement department, the legal department, and the security review committee. A large number of FDE projects that succeeded technically die at these three gates, and die with no technical dignity: contract terms fall through, data processing agreements stall, the security questionnaire reaches its fourth month.

The key to getting through is how you view these three gates: **treat them as obstacles and you'll wreck in the last mile; treat them as part of delivery and you'll actually build advantage**—because most tech companies are cavalier about these three things; if you take them seriously, you win.

The procurement gate: first understand that procurement's metrics are not your enemy. The procurement department's job is to control price, control risk, and ensure compliance. They push back on price not to target you; it's their duty. The counter is not to push back hard, but to give them "tools they can report upward with": a clear phased quote (so discounts have steps), comparable market benchmarks (so approvals have a basis), and most importantly—measurable outcome commitments (so the accusation of "overpaying" can never land). Outcome-based pricing has an unexpected advantage here: the hardest thing for procurement to approve is "spending where you can't articulate what you'll get"; a quote like "pay X dollars per resolved ticket" is something procurement can understand at a glance.

The legal gate: treat the data processing agreement as a product to be honed. The legal focal points of enterprise AI projects are highly concentrated: Is the data used to train models? Where is the data stored, and who can access it? Who pays if there's a security incident? Who is liable when the AI's output is wrong?

Smart teams productize the standard answers to these questions—pre-built agreement templates, clear model usage statements, tiered incident liability frameworks. Palantir's survival in the intelligence world rests on building "permissions and audit" into the product core (who viewed what data, traceable end-to-end); in its work with financial customers, Anthropic sells "traceable, auditable" as an agent design selling point. **Legal trust is designed, not negotiated.**

The security review gate: use a "pre-answered white paper" to claw back two months. The standard move in enterprise security review is a questionnaire of several hundred questions. Wait until the questionnaire arrives to answer it, and each item requires cross-departmental confirmation—measured in months. What efficient teams do is proactively maintain a "security white paper": deployment architecture diagrams, data flow diagrams, encryption and permissions schemes, compliance certifications, and standard answers to every question ever asked in past reviews. Eighty percent of most questionnaires can be copied straight from the white paper. Your response speed itself is the most intuitive signal of security maturity—security teams have seen it all, and they know what a scrambling answer means.

## 3.7 Ecosystem Tie-In: Standing on the Shoulders of Channels

The ecosystem strategy in the FDE era is about borrowing the shoulders of three types of partners.

The first type: cloud vendors and large platforms. AWS, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud are themselves one of the overall entry points for enterprise AI procurement. Getting into their co-sell systems is equivalent to obtaining a pass straight to enterprise procurement lists. The cloud vendors' marketplaces also solve a real pain point: the customer can use existing cloud budget commitments to purchase your service, bypassing the procurement process for adding a new vendor.

The second type: consulting firms and system integrators. This is the most dramatic ecosystem shift of 2026. On the founding partner list of OpenAI's deployment company, there sit side by side Bain, Capgemini, and McKinsey—the world's largest consulting and integration giants, transformed from "potential competitors" into "shareholding allies." The logic is clear: model companies have technology, consulting firms have customer relationships and industry depth, and integrators have delivery manpower—three streams converging is the only way to wholesale capture the giant market of "AI transformation." For startups, the lesson cuts both ways: be wary of consulting giants using ecosystem access to swallow your delivery layer, but also see the real dividend of allying with regional and industry-specific integrators—they hold customer relationships you couldn't build in three years.

The same script played out almost simultaneously in China. In July 2026, Volcano Engine, under ByteDance, and EY, one of the Big Four accounting firms, signed a strategic partnership: to co-build solutions around core scenarios like data governance, financial management, and marketing growth. Most notably, the two sides planned to build a thousand-person FDE team to "create an AI-native delivery team." Volcano Engine president Tan Xingyu put it plainly: the FDE team must "put engineers with both technical and industry backgrounds on the customer's front line, deeply involved in the full process of solution landing." On one side, consulting giants sitting on customer relationships; on the other, model platforms lacking industry depth—FDE became the weld point between them. (Source: Appendix C.)

The third type: the customer's customer. The highest form of tie-in is embedding into the customer's own value chain. After J.D. Power attended Palantir's bootcamp, it started running bootcamps for its own customers—Palantir's capability spilled outward along J.D. Power's customer relationships, and the acquisition cost approached zero. When designing your delivery, you can pre-build this "re-deliverability": can this solution let the customer take it to serve its own customers? If yes, you've gone from vendor to part of the customer's business model—that's the deepest moat.

## 3.8 Scheduling: Who Goes First Is Itself Strategy

Scarcity can create attraction—invitation-only, waitlist mechanisms, all classic plays of internet products. The FDE scheduling problem is opposite in shape but identical in principle: **your capacity is always smaller than demand, so "who goes first" itself becomes a strategic tool.**

First, the brutal real-world constraint. A qualified forward deployment squad (one business-side plus two or three technical-side) can deliver no more than two projects concurrently at high quality. OpenAI started from 2 engineers; Sierra's deployment cycles are measured in months; Harvey's single-firm deployment takes six to nine months—elite delivery capacity is a naturally scarce resource. Scarcity isn't scary; what's scary is having no pricing for the scarcity.

The first principle of scheduling is to rank by strategic value, not by contract value. The decision matrix has only two dimensions: this customer's lighthouse value (industry signal strength) and this customer's learning value (how much reusable capability can accrue). A customer high on both dimensions can and even should be prioritized at a loss—Barry recalls Palantir early on running free pilots and burning millions of dollars, betting on exactly this matrix. A large contract that's low on both dimensions is actually the most dangerous: the money looks big, but it ties up an elite team for half a year.

The second principle is to productize the "wait." If a customer has to wait three months to enter delivery, don't let those three months run dry: give them a data prep checklist (to accelerate on entry), organizational warm-up suggestions (which key people to win first), and a lightweight remote diagnostic (to keep the temperature up and clear mines early). A well-managed waiting period can shorten the delivery cycle by a third after entry—turning the wait from a customer experience burden into a component of delivery quality.

The third principle is to never commit to parallel work beyond capacity. Your delivery quality depends on the same team's continuous focus—each project carries a full load of customer context, and every switch requires a reload; the loss from juggling multiple projects in parallel is far greater than imagined. Better to let sales sell "our next slot is next quarter" as scarcity (which often lets you raise the price) than to let the team scramble between three customers. The better business is, the harder it is to resist taking one more order; and the cost of the extra order blowing up is often far greater than the cost of one fewer order.

## 3.9 Making the Brand Omnipresent with Open-Source Tools and Trial Installs

In the FDE world, there is a unique customer acquisition play: turn what you do well into open-source tools and demos, and let the target customer's engineers start using them first.

The logic: the initiator of enterprise procurement is an executive, but the technical veto is in the engineers' hands. Getting the target customer's engineers to use your stuff in their daily work is equivalent to pre-planting a trust vote on the technical end of the decision chain. Three typical vehicles.

Open-source tools and components. Open-source the general-purpose modules distilled from the delivery process—evaluation frameworks, connectors, deployment templates. Anthropic made the Model Context Protocol (an open standard that lets models connect to external tools) a public specification, and the artifacts FDEs deliver at customer sites are built on top of this protocol—the first lesson a customer engineer learns is the Anthropic tech stack. **The spread of a standard is the accumulation of trust.**

A self-serve "tech showroom." Enterprise products usually don't have self-serve trials, but they can have a tech showroom: public demo environments, sandboxes (isolated practice environments), interactive tutorials. Palantir's bootcamp is essentially "trial" ritualized into an event; for resource-constrained teams, a quick-start package that lets a customer engineer connect sample data and run it through in an afternoon is the best sales engineer.

Presence in the engineer community. In the technical communities of target industries, your engineers should show up consistently—answering questions, sharing hard-won lessons, submitting code. The conversion path from this presence is long, but what it reaches is precisely the people who hold the technical veto. For FDE companies, recruiting and customer acquisition are one and the same in this action: the best candidate pool and the best customer leads often come from the same community.

## 3.10 Writing Proposals and Validation Plans

In the enterprise market, the highest-priced text is the proposal and the proof-of-concept plan—every word worth its weight in gold, no exaggeration.

The bad news is that most technical teams writing proposals make the same mistake: the whole thing talks about "what we will do," not "what you will get." A good FDE proposal follows a strict inverted-pyramid structure.

The first layer: business outcomes, said in one paragraph. The first paragraph of the proposal must be a business outcome in the customer's language: "Within eight weeks, reduce the average handling time of your bank's anti-money-laundering investigations from 4 hours to 15 minutes, freeing up investigation capacity equivalent to 2.5 times the current team." Without this one line, the customer's executives won't read a word further.

The second layer: the value validation path—how you prove it was achieved. Specify acceptance metrics, measurement methods, baseline data, and the exit mechanism at each stage. The signal this layer sends is "we submit to being tested"—in an enterprise market burned by over-promising, the willingness to be tested is the scarcest form of sincerity.

The third layer: the delivery method—how we do it. This layer is where the technical solution finally gets its turn, and the writing should let non-technical readers follow along: architecture diagrams with business annotations, milestones with decision points. Be especially clear about what the customer needs to contribute—data access, key personnel time. Writing out clearly what the customer should do serves two purposes: it clears mines in advance, and it also screens the customer.

The fourth layer: risks and countermeasures—how we've thought through how we might die. Most proposals avoid risks, as if mentioning them is bad luck. The opposite is true: the most mature buyers trust the vendor that "spells out how it dies"—what if data quality falls short, what if key personnel change, what if accuracy misses the threshold. This layer is the proposal's trust amplifier, and the textual expression of Chapter 2's "refuse expensive failures" principle.

Proof-of-concept plans have an additional special quality: they are the carrier of the "graduation standard." The plan must specify the duration cap, the acceptance metrics, and the two follow-on paths—graduate or part ways. A vaguely written validation plan is equivalent to issuing a construction permit for POC purgatory.

## 3.11 From On-Site to Remote: The Boundary of Hybrid Delivery

FDE delivery has gone through an important evolution in recent years: from purely on-site to a "remote-first plus on-site at key milestones" hybrid model. Palantir's official position confirms that many projects now execute mostly remotely, with on-site work concentrated at key milestones.

Designing the rhythm of a hybrid model is a craft. What must be done in person? Experience coalesces into three categories: the early stage of relationship building (first meetings, shadow work method, building trust with executives—trust can't be built through a screen, you have to meet); high-intensity co-creation (bootcamp-style joint construction, whiteboard sieges on key architecture decisions); and politically sensitive periods (solution rollout, cross-department coordination, change management—these moments require you to read the air in the conference room). What's better done remotely? Deep coding, documentation capture, routine iteration—work that needs uninterrupted flow.

The hybrid model has two hidden dividends. One is cost structure: on-site is one of the largest variable items in FDE cost; a reasonable hybrid ratio can pull delivery margin up by more than ten points. The other is talent sustainability: teams that travel half the time year-round have notably higher burnout rates—on practitioner forums, complaints about travel top the list. The hybrid model isn't slacking; it's a safeguard for organizational endurance.

But the bottom line must be drawn: **an FDE that "never meets in person" is a false proposition.** If a project runs entirely remotely from kickoff to delivery, what you lose is not just relationships but the very thing this chapter and Chapter 2 have argued over and over—firsthand cognition of the field. Remote maintains existing trust and context; they must first be created in the physical world.

## 3.12 Overseas Expansion

The FDE model's overseas proposition presents two interesting camps in 2026.

American companies' globalization is demonstrated by the giants: OpenAI's FDE hiring footprint covers San Francisco, New York, Seattle, as well as London, Dublin, Munich, Paris, Zurich, Tokyo, Singapore, Sydney, and Abu Dhabi—the geographic distribution of FDE teams precisely traces the global map of enterprise AI willingness to pay: North America, Western Europe, developed East Asian economies, and Middle Eastern sovereign wealth.

The Chinese context for FDE overseas expansion is a different logic. The old predicament was "product DNA": domestic vendors had long done highly customized, project-based business; their organizational capability didn't match the standardization and productization that overseas markets demand. But the FDE model offers a reverse perspective: **the delivery culture of Chinese engineers—willing to endure hardship, fast to respond, full-stack versatile, accustomed to solving everything on-site at the customer—happens to be the closest fit for what FDE demands.** So the question was never whether Chinese engineers are suited for FDE, but whether the platform base behind them is thick enough. Among the big companies, ByteDance's Volcano Engine set up an FDE team for the Doubao model; among startups, some local teams wrote "FDE ≠ on-site outsourcing" onto their websites—both paths are being explored.

On specific overseas strategy, FDE has one innate advantage and one innate constraint. The advantage: the "lighthouse to endorsement" logic works equally well in global markets, and developed-country enterprise customers' willingness to pay and contract discipline are more mature. The constraint: FDE is a heavily localized business—language, time zone, compliance, and local trust networks each demand a local team, not remote support. This means the cost structure of FDE overseas expansion is inherently higher than that of online software overseas expansion, and the pace must be more restrained: first use products that can be delivered remotely to gain a foothold, then assemble local delivery teams in priority markets, one country at a time.

The twelve things covered in this chapter boil down to one line: **the essence of winning customers is being the trustworthy exception in a market that has been let down countless times.** Signing the contract is just the admission ticket—next, the real hard battle begins: making the system come alive, making the people use it.
