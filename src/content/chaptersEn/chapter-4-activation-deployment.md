## 4.1 Going Live Is Not Activation: The "First-Day Curse" of Enterprise Deployment

First, define the core concept of this chapter. In consumer internet, "activation" means a new user completes a key behavior and experiences the product's "aha moment." In enterprise deployment, activation means **the target user group forms a stable usage habit around the system in their daily work**—not applause at the demo, but the system still being heavily used three months later with no one pushing.

In other words: in enterprise software, "going live" is an administrative event; "activation" is a behavioral event. The first can be celebrated; the second is what counts.

A set of figures from the MIT report is worth laying out again at the opening of this chapter: only about four in ten enterprises offer employees an official AI tool subscription, while as many as nine in ten employees routinely use personal consumer-grade AI tools to get work done. This means a large share of enterprises are in a real state of "dual track": the official system spins idle, shadow AI runs rampant. The system went live, but activation never happened.

Why do enterprise deployments die at the activation stage? Because it has to clear three gates, and they are completely unlike the gates in consumer products. Consumer products die from "not being fun"; enterprise deployments die in the users' hands: users find it awkward—one extra step over the old habit, and no one uses it; users find it untrustworthy—one mistake and trust goes to zero; users find it irrelevant to them—then no one touches it at all. None of these three gates can be solved at headquarters; you can only clear them in the customer's conference rooms, shop floors, and workstations. So activation is the FDE's home turf, and the biggest difference between it and the traditional "deliver and leave" implementation: traditional implementation treats the acceptance form as the finish line; FDE treats the change in customer behavior as the finish line.

The seven sections of this chapter correspond to the seven weapons of activation.

## 4.2 Fast Iteration: The "Hotfix" Culture During Deployment

Internet products iterate through data experiments, taking small steps fast to approach the optimum. In the enterprise deployment scenario you can't run strict data experiments—samples too small, noise too large—but its spiritual core—fast, small-step, evidence-based iteration—becomes a working method during the deployment period: like firefighting a live incident, rapidly responding to every little complaint from users—I call this the "hotfix."

Consumer product iteration cadence is measured in "versions," weekly or biweekly. FDE deployment-period iteration cadence is measured in "days" or even "hours": in the morning a business user says "this output is missing the vendor code field," and by the afternoon the field is added; today the shop-floor manager says "this interface can't be tapped accurately with gloves on," and tomorrow the button is twice as large. This response speed means more to users than the feature itself—**every overnight fix is a top-up of user trust**. Users form a judgment in the first month: "this team is for real," or "another bunch who'll leave once it's handed over."

The hotfix culture has three execution points.

First, feedback must reach the person who writes the code directly, with no telephone game in between. Before, user feedback had to pass through customer success, the product manager, the schedule, and only then reach the engineer—each handoff loses half the context, and by the time it's scheduled a week has passed and the user's heart has gone cold. Under the FDE model, feedback reaches the person writing the code directly; ideally that person is sitting right next to the user. What Palantir shortens is exactly this loop: the forward deployed engineer can build, test, learn, and feed back on-site, without waiting for a formal product requirement to run its course.

Second, iteration priority is ranked by "usage-blocking degree," not "feature importance." The trade-off logic during deployment is the reverse of the product phase. In the product phase, requirements are ranked by strategic value; in the deployment phase the question is "what is blocking tomorrow's usage"—a color scheme problem that makes shop-floor workers feel "this was made for office white-collars" is the highest priority; a powerful predictive feature that users can't use right now gets pushed to after activation is complete. Win usage first, then win depth.

Third, at the end of each day ask one question: what did today's changes make smoother in some moment of the user's tomorrow? Fixing this way is no longer just "responding to every request," but actively clearing every friction point between the user and the system. A fix is not just a defect fix—every fix is a deliberate act of activation design: you are dismantling, one by one, the friction points between the user and the system. The list of friction points is hidden in the process map from the entry due diligence and in daily observation.

OpenAI's FDE methodology has a corresponding cadence division: early co-creation (on-site whiteboard alignment), validation (build an evaluation system), delivery (multi-day on-site build). Note the delivery phase is still measured in "multi-day on-site" units—being able to fix this fast relies on a person staying next to the problem.

## 4.3 Iterate Inside the Customer's Environment: Evaluation-System-Driven Quality Improvement

If 4.2 is about the "speed" of iteration, this section is about the "sense of direction" of iteration. In AI deployment, the carrier of that sense of direction is a practice that only became mainstream after 2024: **evaluation systems**—using a set of standard cases to continuously score the system's outputs.

Traditional software quality has only two states: the feature is right or wrong, tested or not tested. AI system quality is continuous, probabilistic, and context-dependent—the same answer that dazzles in a demo may be a disaster in a specific business context. Worse, the right to define "good" lies with the business side, not the engineering side: a model that considers its answer perfect may be spotted as amateur by a business expert at a glance. Without this evaluation yardstick, iteration in AI deployment is running blind—you think you're optimizing, but you're actually drifting randomly.

The engineering practice of evaluation systems has already solidified into three steps among leading teams.

Step one: grow it out of real cases. The evaluation set cannot be fabricated by engineers; it must come from the customer's real business corpus. The essence of this step is to take the business expert's tacit judgment and make it explicit as an executable yardstick.

Step two: make the business side the judges. The evaluation system is not an engineer's solo tool; the reviewers must include the business side. The best approach is to shape evaluation into a form that business experts can participate in: side-by-side output comparison, simple good/bad labels, regular review meetings. This process yields a double return: the evaluation set gets more and more accurate, and the business side's understanding of the system gets deeper and deeper—they watch the system do better and better on their own cases, and the accumulation of trust is seen with their own eyes, not obtained from a report.

Step three: wire the evaluation system into the production loop. Go-live is not the end of evaluation. In the production environment, continuously collect real inputs and outputs, sample and evaluate on a regular schedule, and alert immediately when scores slip—this turns "quality" from a one-time acceptance before go-live into continuous guardianship throughout the lifecycle. What users are most sensitive to is not average quality, but the stability of quality: a single mistake by the system causes a trust collapse that takes ten correct answers to fill back in.

John Deere's collaboration is the most complete public sample of "evaluation system first," worth slowing down to examine. The company wants to solve herbicide waste: traditional sprayers cover the whole field, while its "see-and-spray" technology uses 36 cameras plus machine vision, traveling at 12 to 15 miles per hour, to spray only the weeds—covering three football fields' worth per minute. The ambition of precision agriculture is large: the United States plants 12 trillion stalks of corn and soybeans each year, the best farmland yields 200 bushels per acre, top growers can hit 600—"if every single stalk could be tended individually, yields could be turned upside down." This is a direct quote from Justin Rose, John Deere's technology executive.

But what farmers want is not technology, it's trustworthy advice. OpenAI's forward deployed engineers flew to Iowa, followed agronomists into the fields, first reviewed hundreds of real operational cases with the experts, built a custom evaluation system, and only then rapidly iterated the model—on a schedule dictated by the farming calendar: miss planting season and you miss a whole year. The final result: chemical usage reduced by up to 70%, and farmer interaction frequency increased sixfold. Note the order of these two numbers: **first the "good" defined by the evaluation system, then the "good" delivered by the model.** (Source: Appendix C.)

The deeper significance of the evaluation system is this, plainly stated: it turns the veteran craftsman's notion of "what counts as good" inside his head into a scoring standard the system executes automatically every day. This is exactly the microcosm of the FDE model—customer knowledge is no longer just words in a requirements document, but a yardstick alive inside the system.

## 4.4 Find Another Way: Lower the Usage Barrier

Making users reach value with the fewest actions is a product-design truism. But the usage barrier for enterprise systems is often an obstacle outside the system itself. The "barrier-lowering" methods repeatedly validated in deployment practice come in four forms.

Method one: parasitize inside the user's existing interface. Wherever the user's main battlefield is, that's where your system should appear: if they work in spreadsheets, you make a spreadsheet plugin; if they approve in email, you let approval happen inside email; if they work in a ticket system, you embed AI suggestions into the ticket card. Asking them to log into a new system is the same as building a wall between you and them that they have to climb every day. OpenAI cut into BBVA's 120,000 employees through the existing ChatGPT interface, and Anthropic lets its model enter customers' existing workflow tools through open protocols—both are the same logic. **Every extra login costs you a slice of activation rate.**

BBVA is the best sample of "entering the organization along the old habit." It began working with OpenAI in May 2024, and the first step was to issue only 3,300 ChatGPT Enterprise accounts—not a company-wide campaign, but letting seed users play on their own first. Employees soon spontaneously created over 20,000 custom mini-agents, of which about 4,000 were heavily used; management did not ban "shadow AI," but did the opposite: "We give everyone a safe platform and let them try with peace of mind." Alongside this came structured training: 250 executives (including the chairman himself) took classes first, the whole bank built an "AI Pioneer Network," and cultivated a batch of advanced users internally called "AI geeks." The data more than a year later: employees saved an average of about 3 hours per week, and 83% were active weekly. In December 2025, the two sides announced a full-bank rollout as a natural next step: 25 countries, 120,000 employees, and the launch of an end-to-end transformation roadmap called "Eight Things." From 3,300 to 11,000 to 120,000, every step of expansion happened after the previous step's usage data was published—this is activation by "letting the habit walk on its own." (Source: Appendix C.)

Method two: activation rate hides inside defaults. The first reaction of a new user facing a blank system is "and then what," and most churn happens in those three seconds. The FDE's solution is to pre-install the "first use": prefabricated templates, pre-filled examples (based on the customer's own data), preset guidance (the first time they open it, there's already a to-do item that belongs to them). The system on day one should already understand better than the user what they might need to do—what you observed during the first few days of stationing on-site comes in handy here.

Method three: translate "ask the AI" into "press a button." Enterprise users' fluency with "having a conversation with AI" is far more uneven than imagined. Making users organize their own prompts is shifting the engineering burden onto the people least equipped to bear it. The mature approach is to package high-frequency scenarios into one-click actions: "generate last week's anomaly report," "reconcile this batch of invoices," "draft this customer letter"—behind the button is a complete, evaluation-tuned prompt and workflow. Conversational interaction is for exploration; button interaction is for daily use.

Method four: be the "copilot" first, then talk about "autopilot." For high-risk, high-resistance processes, don't push automation all at once. Let the system exist first in the role of "adviser"—AI drafts, human confirms; AI flags, human adjudicates. Users build a calibrated trust in the system's judgment through round after round of confirmation; only when the confirmation pass rate gets high enough does automation go on the agenda. John Deere's solution still preserves the agronomist's final decision authority; agent design in financial-compliance scenarios generally retains a human checkpoint. The copilot strategy isn't conservatism—it's the optimal path between activation rate and risk.

## 4.5 The Long, Grinding War of Integration

Every FDE veteran is covered in scars, and they all come from the same war: the integration war against the customer's "legacy systems"—those old systems that have run inside enterprises for years, that no one dares touch.

The brutality of this war exceeds the imagination of anyone who hasn't spent time on the front line. a16z's description hits the nail on the head: the context AI applications need—historical records, business logic, permission systems—is all locked inside the enterprise's internal databases, interfaces, and workflows, and the work of connecting them "is never an elective, it's a required course." Old mainframes in finance, heterogeneous systems across hundreds of clinics in healthcare, dozens of shop-floor systems each going their own way in manufacturing—what these environments share is this: documentation is outdated, interfaces are incomplete, and half the people who understood them have already retired.

There are four strategic points to this war.

- **Fight integration as a campaign, don't handle it as a chore.** Integration work in most project plans gets written as a single line—"system integration: 2 weeks"—and then in execution balloons into four months. The root of the error is treating integration as a technical chore—it is at once technical archaeology, organizational politics, and data governance all in one; it is a hard war, plain and simple. The right posture: draw the system map during entry due diligence, rank integration risks by priority, and chew the hardest bone earliest—integration has the most uncertainty, and every day you delay starting, the whole project's timeline runs naked for a day.

- **Second, solve the data problem before the model problem.** That report contains a widely-cited insight: the underlying cause of a large share of AI project failures is "garbage in, garbage out"—the AI taps into an ungoverned data source, the same document exists in ten versions and gets hit at random, and the output is naturally untrustworthy. Palantir's solution is the Ontology: first model the enterprise's data assets into a layer that carries business semantics, sorting out "which field is authoritative, which version is current, who is allowed to see what," and AI runs on top of this foundation. The universal lesson of this path is: **data governance is not a prerequisite of the AI project—it is the AI project itself.**

The U.S. Navy's "shipbuilding operating system" is the most spectacular footnote to this lesson. In December 2025, the Secretary of the Navy and Palantir's CEO jointly announced a $448 million contract: first covering two large shipyards, three naval shipyards, and a hundred suppliers. The data environment in shipbuilding is a textbook-grade disaster: enterprise resource planning systems, decades-old databases, and paper blueprints all coexist. And the two numbers from the pilot phase made everyone shut up: at nuclear-submarine manufacturer General Dynamics Electric Boat, **submarine production scheduling was compressed from 160 person-hours to under 10 minutes**; at Portsmouth Naval Shipyard, **material review time was compressed from weeks to under an hour**.

The Secretary of the Navy emphasized specifically: "This is not a concept, not a pilot, not a study—this is already being done." (Source: Appendix C.) Note the order of this case: first spend heavy effort getting the data into a unified semantic layer, and only then can the efficiency miracle happen—not the other way around.

The same logic reappears in three completely different industries. Fast-food chain Wendy's: syrup stockout scheduling across 6,450 North American stores used to take 15 employees a full day checking; after tapping into the data platform, a plan comes out in five minutes. Mortgage giant Fannie Mae: using AI to identify mortgage fraud, the detection rate exceeds 99%, far surpassing the old rule-based system. Citi: customer credit approval went from hours down to minutes—AI reads through credit history, transaction habits, industry risk, and affiliated entities in one pass. Three industries, one common precondition: first get the scattered data into a semantic layer machines can understand, and only then does intelligence have a place to land. (Source: Appendix C.)

- **Third, use AI to fight the integration war of AI.** A major variable post-2025: the integration work itself is starting to be automated by AI. The scenario a16z envisioned has already partly become reality—old systems with no interface get scraped by browser agents simulating humans fetching data; field mapping, format conversion, and interface-document interpretation are increasingly handed to models. The leading teams' self-imposed bar is worth quoting: "Automate the integration process as much as possible—process mining, data pipelines, system hookup, interface-document teasing—this speed advantage compounds." Using AI to do the work of AI deployment may be the most delightful part of this role.

- **Fourth, know when to bypass rather than conquer.** Not every legacy system is worth integrating head-on. The correct solution for some is "shadow read" (read-only snapshot, nightly sync), for some is "human ferry" (keep a manual step during transition), and for some is simply "declare isolation" (that process's data is out of scope for this project, state it explicitly to the customer). Engineer pride always wants to conquer every fortress; the FDE's judgment shows up in choosing the battlefield—remember, your goal is not a technical clean sweep, it's the customer's outcome.

## 4.6 Change Management: Get the Customer Organization to Stand Up for You

This section deals with activation's biggest soft resistance: the organization. In the report's five major roadblocks, "employee resistance" and "change management" together account for nearly half. Technical go-live only needs engineers; behavioral change needs the whole organization.

Change management in the FDE context is, at its core, the management of three sets of people.

- **The sponsor | your internal ally:** Behind every successful deployment stands a sponsor inside the customer: someone who truly believes in this thing and is willing to stake their reputation on clearing the path for you. David Wakeling at Linklaters is a textbook example—as the firm's head of market innovation, he was Harvey's in-house counterpart, advocate, and umbrella. The points of sponsor management: give him material he can tell (stories he can tell outward), give him battle honors (turn his vision into highlights of his career), give him safety (you step in front when things fail). A well-treated sponsor is worth more than ten product pitch sessions.

- **The influencer | the informal opinion leader:** Every organization has a batch of uncrowned kings: the senior analyst, the veteran craftsman on the shop floor, the person in the department who is "just ask him." They hold no power, but they hold trust. Their one sentence of "this thing actually works" outweighs three all-staff emails from management; their one sentence of "window dressing" is enough to make the system die quietly at the grassroots. During activation, deliberately manage this group: invite them to be the first batch of trial users, take every one of their gripes seriously, make the adoption of their suggestions visible—"this field was added per Master Wang's input." Making influencers co-authors is the shortest path to cracking the "if I didn't make it, I won't use it" mindset.

- **The loser | the interest group the solution disturbs:** Automation inevitably redistributes work, and redistributing work inevitably creates losers: the approval role whose sense of presence has been compressed, the department whose information barrier has been penetrated, the veteran employee whose "secret craft" has been replaced. Ignore them, and they become the system's most stubborn underground resistance—passive non-cooperation, spreading incident cases, voting against at acceptance. Mature change management designs "a way out for the losers" in advance: redirect the freed-up manpower toward higher-value work (and publicly commit to no layoffs), turn "gatekeepers" into "coaches" (the veteran's experience is used to train the system), and let the losers see their own place in the future. This is both humane and pure utility—the cost of resistance is far higher than the cost of appeasement.

The ticketing platform Vivid Seats' partnership with Sierra shows what "all-in mobilization on the customer side" looks like during activation. After deciding to bring in an agent, the company threw its product, customer experience, and engineering teams all in—"Once the decision was made, we were one hundred percent in, everyone retesting." Result: from kickoff to go-live in under four weeks, post-launch self-resolution rate of issues rose 40%, and customer satisfaction rose 35%.

But the more valuable part was what came after: once routine questions were taken over by the agent, the customer experience team shifted from "queuing to fight fires" to fixing root-process problems, and even had room for delight projects like "surprise upgrades"; and the agent's conversation data started feeding back into the product—"if ten thousand people ask about the same feature in a month, we can immediately prioritize it. The best moment is when a rule-driven product improvement means users don't need to ask for help at all anymore." (Source: Appendix C.) **The highest state of activation is for the system to make itself unnecessary**—this case also previews Chapter 7's "the field feeding back into the product."

A corresponding sample from a Chinese company is also emerging. Ren Xiliang, head of efficiency engineering at the e-commerce company Dewu, breaks the AI transformation of a ten-thousand-person company into three steps: step one, reach consensus across the whole company, lower the tool barrier, and get everyone using it first; step two, divide the company's scenarios into four quadrants by error-tolerance room, and hand the high-tolerance scenarios (like operating analysis) to AI first; step three, set up a dedicated knowledge-operations team that burrows into the business teams and moves the expert's tacit experience out of individual heads and into a space visible to AI. Consensus, scenarios, knowledge—the order cannot be reversed: first get people willing to use it, then pick the right place to use it, and last give AI grain to cook with. (Source: Appendix C.)

The ultimate test of change management has only one criterion: **after your team withdraws, is the system still being used.** If it's bustling while you're there and goes cold fast after you leave, that's not activation—that's a dance-along. An organization that's truly activated walks forward on its own.

## 4.7 I, Robot—Automating the Delivery Work Itself

Delivering automation to the customer is the product; automating your own delivery work is efficiency. This section is about the latter—it's the first lever that lets an FDE team escape "revenue growing linearly with headcount."

In an FDE's daily work, an astonishing proportion of actions is templatable, repetitive labor: a new customer's deployment-environment initialization, the standard pipeline for data onboarding, questionnaire responses for security review, the pre-go-live checklist, the periodic report format sent to the customer. Every action done a second time should trigger a question: "Can this become a script, a template, or a checklist?"

What leading teams do has already settled into four categories of assets.

- **Deployment templates:** Package environment setup, permissions configuration, and monitoring hookup into one-click infrastructure code. A new customer walks in, and on day one a standardized environment is spun up in the customer's cloud, rather than configuring from scratch for a week. Decagon can compress deployment for simple scenarios to 15 days, and behind that is a highly templated onboarding layer. (Source: Appendix C.)

- **Integration component library:** Connectors for mainstream enterprise systems (prebuilt system-hookup components), written once and reused everywhere. Palantir's Ontology model essentially takes this logic to its extreme: the product of data onboarding is not a throwaway pipeline, but a reusable, business-meaningful data asset.

- **Checklist culture:** Security review checklists, go-live readiness checklists, withdrawal handover checklists. The checklist is humanity's oldest tool against complexity, and the bedrock of an FDE team's quality stability—it turns "dependent on individual experience" into "dependent on organizational memory."

- **Automated reporting:** Weekly reports to the customer, field intelligence to the company, should both be semi-automatically generated. An FDE's hourly rate is too high to spend on copy-paste—much less let "forgot to report" sever the loop between the field and the product that Chapter 7 will cover.

How large the compounding of automation gets can be corroborated from one angle: a16z lists "build or buy tools to automate service delivery" as a key recommendation for setting up a forward deployed team, and judges it to be the key variable that lets this generation of AI companies run faster and clear a lower deal-size threshold than the last generation of enterprise-software companies. Sierra's fastest publicly reported go-live case is four weeks; Palantir compresses the sales cycle from nine months to weeks—neither relies on engineers working overtime, but on turning yesterday's delivery into today's scaffolding.

This section also answers the most common challenge—"Isn't FDE just a human-wave tactic?" The essence of a human wave isn't that there are many people, but that every person is doing non-reusable one-off labor. **When every delivery of an FDE team is paving the road for the next delivery, it is no longer a human wave—it is a machine accelerating itself.**

That concludes the seven weapons of activation and deployment: the speed of hotfixes, the direction of evaluation systems, the craft of lowering barriers, the patience of the integration war, the soft craft of change management, the leverage of automation. The system is alive, and the people are moving. But the cruelty of the enterprise business is this: activation is only a lease on life; renewal is survival. Next chapter, defending the renewal.
