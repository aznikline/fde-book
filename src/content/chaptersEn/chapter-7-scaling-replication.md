## 7.1 Using Replicability as the Lever of Delivery

The highest form of internet growth is one user bringing in more users—the growth engine shifts from bought to organic, from acquired to homegrown. The question FDE has to answer is the same: **after every customer, the next customer should be easier to deliver—not by adding people, but by banking the last experience.**

This proposition is the "last mile" of the whole book, because it is the last line of defense between the FDE model and the consulting and outsourcing industries. Take this chapter out of the first six chapters' playbook, and what's the ending? An elite team that fights hard battles, takes one customer, delivers one customer, earns enough to cover costs, then takes the next—revenue growth perfectly linear with headcount growth. Congratulations, you've reinvented the consulting firm.

Barry put this dividing line most ruthlessly in his memoir: "If you don't convert field learning into product assets, all you get is a pile of custom projects." McGrew's formulation comes from another angle: the hallmark of a working FDE model is "declining customization per subsequent customer"—if the customization you do doesn't decrease as the customer count rises, you're not doing FDE, you're doing a person-day business.

The leverage of replicability has three levels, each harder than the last, and each more valuable than the last.

- **Level-one leverage | knowledge replication (playbook):** Write the "how" into documents—playbook templates, checklists, training materials. It turns dependence on individual experience into dependence on organizational memory: a new hire's ramp-up period compresses from a year to three months. This is the most basic leverage; most teams stop here.

- **Level-two leverage | component replication (tools and code):** Turn "what's been done" into "what can be used directly"—integration connectors, evaluation frameworks, deployment templates, industry data models. The next team's starting point is no longer zero, but the shoulders of the previous project. The automated assets discussed in Section 4.7 belong to this level.

- **Level-three leverage | product replication (platform capability):** Abstract "repeatedly recurring customization" into a standard platform capability—from then on, this requirement needs no engineer on site. Palantir's Ontology, Decagon's agent-flow configuration, and Sierra's agent platform are all products of level-three leverage. What this level of leverage changes is the cost structure of the whole business: **the first two levels of leverage lower delivery cost, the third level eliminates delivery cost**.

And the progression of these three levels is the complete version of McGrew's "gravel roads and highways": FDE builds gravel roads (solves the problem in front of it), the component team lays the crushed-stone base (makes the next car ride easier), the platform team pours the highway (from then on anyone can pass). The remaining seven sections of this chapter describe this complete engineering, from road-building to paving.

## 7.2 Bad News Travels Fast—Spreading the Lessons of Failed Deployments

Turning incidents into assets is an organization's scarce capability. The FDE organization's attitude toward failure determines whether it can possess replicability: **failure is the raw material of replication; an organization that covers up failure is burying a gold mine as garbage**.

One thing first: in the FDE model, failure isn't an accident, it's a certainty. Barry's recollection is unflinching: "There was plenty of failure—gobs of time, money, and travel expenses, burned spectacularly." Palantir simply wrote acceptance of failure into the system: treat pilots like venture capital bets, most will die, and once dead, must be reviewed. He specifically reminds us: to the projects that crashed, the teams that worked for nothing, the engineers who burned out, also say thank you—the lessons were paid for out of their pocket. This is the hardest to learn, because engineers' instinct is to celebrate success and hide failure.

Three mechanisms are needed to turn failure into an organizational asset.

- **De-stigmatizing the post-mortem:** The sole rule of a post-mortem meeting: attack the problem, not the person. The moment post-mortems are tied to performance, people start polishing failures—and polished failures have zero teaching value. De-stigmatization isn't "no accountability"; it's redefining "accountability" as "whether the lessons were extracted honestly and completely." A failed-project lead who runs an honest post-mortem may contribute more to the organization than a project that succeeded by luck.

- **Structuring the lessons:** Post-mortem output can't be "let's be careful next time" sentiments; it must be retrievable, executable knowledge: what are the high-risk signals for this customer type? Which integration assumption got overturned? Which evaluation metric was designed wrong? These conclusions should go into the "minefield" section of the playbook and into the diligence checklist for new projects—so the next team's "entry diligence" automatically carries historical lessons. **Failure has only been truly absorbed when it's written into the process**.

- **Moderate external sharing of failure:** This layer is the most counterintuitive: sharing failure lessons externally (after anonymization) is extremely effective trust marketing. As discussed in Section 3.5, Barry's "airing our own dirty laundry" article became the best evangelism for the Palantir model—readers can tell who's peddling booth-talk and who's telling trench-truth. A team that dares to publicly dissect its failures sends the signal "we've already paid tuition for this industry's detours"—exactly what enterprise customers most want to hear.

## 7.3 Riding the Tailwind: Standing in the Industry's Wind

Using the attention around external events for your own purposes is a classic market-making move. Teams on the FDE track are standing in an epic updraft, and the updraft's window won't stay open forever.

The industry updraft of 2025 into 2026 is formed by several air currents converging: that report turned the "95% failure rate" into a recurring nightmare for every enterprise CIO—the customer's pain is your opportunity; OpenAI and Anthropic founded deployment companies on the same day, pushing "deployment" onto the financial headlines—the money the giants spend educating the market benefits the whole industry; the 800% hiring growth of FDE roles made this position a regular on tech-media—such that even "should we hire an FDE?" has become a kind of corporate anxiety. These three currents together produced a rare situation: **customers are actively looking for you, discussing the very thing you want to sell**.

The first layer of riding the tailwind is "defining the agenda." The attention of an updraft is a public resource; whoever's content defines the agenda harvests the attention. a16z set the tone for the entire track with one landmark essay; communities like OpenFDE aggregate practitioners and, at the same time, aggregate definitional authority. For a specific company, the best way to seize definitional authority over the agenda is to bring out the bottom-of-the-drawer stuff: playbooks, failure post-mortems, value data—exclusive first-hand insight is the only currency of agenda-defining authority.

The second layer is "binding to the industry agenda." The biggest driver of enterprise AI purchasing is upgrading from "efficiency" to "survival"—CEOs are being pressed in boardrooms with "what's our AI strategy," and this anxiety transmits into budget. Translate your solution into "boardroom language": not "we can help you optimize your customer-service workflow," but "we can let you, at the next board meeting, produce a quantifiable AI scorecard." The same delivery, anchored to different agenda levels, reaches different budget pools.

The third layer is "building assets during the updraft, rather than burning through it." The updraft will stop. The discussion stirred up by the report will cool; the media's buzzwords will go out of fashion. The most valuable thing in the updraft period isn't signing a few more deals, but converting the attention brought by the updraft into long-term assets: lighthouse cases, the discourse authority on industry methodology, replicable delivery systems. **After the updraft passes, the skinny-dippers recede with the tide; the asset-holders make landfall**.

## 7.4 Building the Customer-External Propagation Loop

Propagation that relies on word of mouth rather than advertising happens in this profession mainly through three circles.

Circle one: the practitioner community. FDE is a professional community in formation—in 2025 it was still scattered inside various companies; by 2026 there were dedicated communities like OpenFDE, dedicated compensation reports, dedicated interview guides. The formation of a professional community is a godsend dividend for first movers: contribute content to the community (methodology, tools, data), and your name becomes bound to this emerging profession. The propagation path of concepts has always been this way: the early evangelists ultimately became synonymous with the concept.

Circle two: the customer's industry community. Every industry has its own small circle—bankers' forums, law-firm partners' annual meetings, hospital administrators' summits. The trust dividend discussed in Section 3.3 flows mainly inside these closed circles. The ticket into these circles isn't advertising; it's a customer supporter invited to speak—your job is to give him something worth talking about and worth telling well. The ten minutes a customer spends speaking on your behalf at his industry summit beat a hundred days of your booth at an industry trade show.

Circle three: the ecosystem-partner network. Section 3.7 covered the acquisition value of ecosystem bundling; here we emphasize its propagation property: the cloud vendor's solutions architect, the consulting firm's consultant, the integrator's project manager—these people flow between different customer sites every day. The "reliable team" list in their mouths is the fastest, most accurate promotional channel. The key to cultivating this circle is to let them "win": let ecosystem partners gain performance, cases, and word of mouth in their collaboration with you. **People who help others succeed are remembered by the whole network**.

The flywheel formed by the three circles together is: delivery creates stories, stories propagate through the circles, propagation brings new opportunities, new opportunities create new stories. The difference between this flywheel and traditional marketing is that its fuel is entirely "already-delivered value"—the essence of word-of-mouth propagation is that every ounce of past effort pulls in customers for the future.

## 7.5 Viral Factors Built into the Product

Good products propagate themselves—that line in the email signature, "sent from some-email-client," was the earliest self-propagating design. The systems FDE delivers can also pre-embed "built-in diffusion mechanisms," letting the system itself become its own salesman for expansion.

Factor one: visibility design. A system's outputs are naturally passed around—weekly reports, analysis results, approval flows. Have every output carry its "provenance": the report footer's "generated by such-and-such system," the processing-entry link inside the anomaly alert. When a beautiful analysis report is forwarded to the next department's director, the people who see it will naturally ask "what's this made with." The essence of visibility design is letting the system's value exhibit itself in the daily workflow, rather than relying on special reporting.

Factor two: collaborative access. Design features that require cross-role collaboration: the investigator's flagged cases need supervisor review, the analyst's report needs the business side's confirmation—every collaborative action naturally introduces the system to a new user. New users brought in by collaboration aren't "marketed to"; they're "brought in by the work itself," and the acceptance rate is completely different.

Factor three: a self-service exploration layer. Outside the core delivery, leave a low-risk practice area (a sandbox): the customer's employees can try "let AI help me run an analysis" on their own. The uses that grow out of the sandbox are often the most vital expansion leads—a scenario an employee in some department plays out himself has more roots than a scenario the sales team pushes ten times. **Let everyone do discovery for you; let FDE do the hard assaults**.

Two 2026 products turned this factor into an open card. In March, Sierra launched "Ghostwriter": the customer uploads its own standard operating procedures, historical conversation logs, even a whiteboard photo, or describes a goal in plain language, and the system auto-generates a production-ready agent, covering voice, chat, and email across more than thirty languages—customer business staff can "self-serve build an Agent" for the first time, while the forward deployed team shifts from "hand-building every one" to "acceptance and backstop." Decagon's agent operation flow walked this path earlier: the customer's customer-service supervisor writes the flow in natural language ("returns over 30 days, first check membership tier, process if it fits policy"), no need to wait on an engineer's schedule—reportedly, Chime's self-service resolution rate reached 70%, Duolingo's problem triage rate hit 80%, and the fitness platform ClassPass cut customer-service costs by 95%. (Source: Appendix C.) **When customers start building on their own, your role shifts from construction crew to design institute**—exactly the terminal form of factor three.

Factor four: cross-boundary evidence of value. Auto-generate a "this-quarter value report" every quarter, and let it flow naturally to the customer's executive layer. This report is both the renewal engineering of Chapter 5 and a stepping stone for vertical expansion—every number an executive sees lays the groundwork for "where else this system is worth using."

The common principle of the built-in factors is: **to get others to propagate for you, you must first let them actually derive value**. Any design that propagates for propagation's sake will, in the enterprise setting, be identified as "vendor's little trick" and resented. There's no viral propagation in the enterprise market—only the "chain reaction of word of mouth"—slow, but every step solid.

## 7.6 Grasping Organizational Politics During Scaling

The biggest obstacle to scaling is never technology; it's human turf.

When a system expands from one department to ten, you are effectively rewriting the power map inside the organization: information monopolies get broken (data once hoarded by a department is now visible company-wide), approval authority gets compressed (what AI answers in seconds no longer needs three days of layer-by-layer sign-off), professional barriers get leveled (the senior employee's "secret expertise" gets encoded into the system). Every one of these changes produces silent enemies.

To deal with organizational politics, FDE veterans have settled on three precepts.

- **Precept one: expansion must bring "locals" along.** On entering any new department, first find that department's "power broker"—the most respected senior employee—and invite him into the adaptation process: "For this scenario, what's different in your department?" His answers make the solution more accurate; his participation makes the solution unimpeded on his turf. **People don't oppose what they helped build**—the most reliable lever in organizational behavior.

Harvey's expansion into top-tier law firms is a classic teaching case for reading organizational politics. The firm simultaneously holds three layers of people: partners—the ones who pay, but don't do the work themselves; paralegals—the ones who do the work, but don't sign off; knowledge-management lawyers—neither here nor there, yet they can see both sides' blind spots at once. A generic questionnaire survey would compress these three layers into the same set of drop-down options and yield a pile of distorted averages; Harvey's forward deployed team designed separate interviews and demos for each layer: show the partners "how much more money this deal makes," show the paralegals "three fewer days of overtime this week," show the knowledge-management lawyers "the firm's tacit knowledge finally has a place to settle." One deployment has to make all three layers feel like beneficiaries at once—miss any layer, and the system gets quietly shelved at the corresponding handoff. (Source: Appendix C.)

- **Precept two: every step of expansion must have a "beneficiary."** Before expanding, calculate clearly what each relevant department gets: the department whose data barrier was punctured gets globally-visible analytical capability; the department whose approval authority was compressed gets the right to focus on exception items (AI handles the routine, humans handle the hard cases). Expansion without a beneficiary is aggression; expansion with a beneficiary is liberation—the only difference is whether you've thought through and articulated "what he gets."

- **Precept three: always leave the "old order" its dignity.** The process replaced by the system was once designed by someone competent; the experience encoded was once some expert's life's work. In the rollout messaging, respect for the old order is respect for the old order's maintainers: "This process supported the company for the past decade; now we're going to amplify its ten years of experience a hundredfold." An old world treated with dignity won't make trouble in the new world.

## 7.7 Using the Playbook to Bank Replication Efficiency

At the start of this chapter the playbook was listed as level-one leverage; this section unfolds its engineering details—because most teams' playbooks end up as "dead-on-arrival" document graveyards.

- **The first attribute of the playbook: it grows in the process, not lying in the knowledge base.** A playbook that can only be found by searching the documentation system might as well not exist. The effective practice is to embed it in the workflow: at the diligence stage of a new project, the system auto-pops up the diligence checklist for that customer type; at the deployment stage, the checklist is a hard gate in the task system; at the post-mortem stage, playbook updates are a mandatory post-mortem deliverable. **Knowledge that isn't embedded in process is just archaeological material**.

- **The second attribute of the playbook: organize by "scenario," not by "function."** Failed playbooks are written by company org structure ("delivery process," "technical spec"); successful playbooks are written by customer scenario ("financial anti-money-laundering playbook," "manufacturing scheduling playbook," "law-firm knowledge-base playbook"). Each scenario playbook contains a fixed seven-piece set: typical pain points and fit-check essentials, diligence checklist (the high-risk signals unique to this scenario), known data-and-integration minefields, evaluation-and-acceptance metric templates, change-management role maps, pricing-and-expansion reference structures, and a lesson library from historical post-mortems. Harvey's ability to replicate its law-firm scenario playbook to PwC and Cleary Gottlieb hinged on writing the first customer's (Linklaters') experience into an inheritable scenario asset.

- **The third attribute of the playbook: it must be "alive"—with an owner, a version, and depreciation.** Each scenario playbook has a designated owner (usually the most senior delivery team); after each project, a forced look-back-and-update; every six months, an overall overhaul—deleting what's outdated, merging what's duplicated. A playbook with no owner will rot within a year into a trap that misleads newcomers, worse than no playbook at all.

The relationship between the playbook and level-two leverage (the component library), and level-three leverage (the platform) is: the playbook records "judgment," the component library deposits "craft," and the platform fixes "capability." With all three in place, a newly-formed FDE squad can reach 80% of the veteran team's combat power in two weeks—that's the true meaning of replication.

## 7.8 The Planning and Polishing of Productization

The final section covers the summit work of level-three leverage: crystallizing field experience into a product—the alchemy of the entire FDE model.

First, the most spectacular success case: Palantir's Foundry. Today this platform, with annual revenue in the billions of dollars and supporting the company's hundreds-of-billions market cap, had its core components born in customer sites scattered across Zurich, Houston, São Paulo, Toulouse, Baku, and other far-flung places—tools that forward deployed engineers in each place built to solve local problems, then after years of natural selection and platform-level assimilation, ultimately grew into a unified product. Barry recalls the process as "entirely bottom-up": in 2014 the company's product strategy was literally "strong opinions, loosely held"—let a hundred flowers bloom in the field, and whatever survived got brought into the core.

This process isn't a pastoral idyll; it has a severe internal logic, which can be distilled into "the four questions of productization."

- **Question one:** Is this field solution "personality" or "commonality"? The test: has the same kind of problem appeared independently at three or more customers? The same pain point across three customers is a product signal; a special requirement at one customer might just be a quirk. Palantir's rule of thumb is to let "multiple forward deployed teams reinventing the same wheel" naturally expose commonality—when three teams each build something similar, the platform team knows: it's time to assimilate.

- **Question two:** Is the cost of generalization less than the gain? McGrew reminded us: field code is "fast and rough," and the cost of generalizing it "is often higher than writing the first version." A productization decision must settle this account: generalization investment, against the custom-delivery cost saved across future customers. What doesn't pay off stays at the component layer—don't force it onto the platform. Every capability in the platform is a lifelong maintenance commitment.

- **Question three:** Can it be used by "someone who can't write code"? The user of a field tool is an engineer; the user of a platform capability is everyone. The hardest step of productization is often not technical but interaction: turn the engineer's script into a function the business person can configure. Decagon's agent-flow configuration (letting operations define flows in natural language) is the model for this step—get the abstraction layer right, and the user base of the capability expands a hundredfold.

- **Question four:** After assimilation, is there still room on the field? This is the most delicate balance: assimilate too much into the platform, and the field team degrades into configurators—the soul of the FDE model (field creativity) dies; assimilate too little, and the leverage of scaling can't get up. Palantir's solution is to maintain "radical authorization of the front to the base"—the top sets only the objective; the playbook belongs to the field. **The platform makes "the common thing easy"; the field makes "the impossible thing happen"**.

For those who doubt this flywheel, the most powerful rebuttal is always gross margin: how can sending people to the field produce high margins? History has answered three times. ServiceNow's IPO-time gross margin was 63.2%, criticized as "too much like a services company"—a decade later, 79%, with a market cap near $200 billion; Workday was 54.1% at IPO—now 76%; Palantir was 79% at IPO, and reached 82% in 2025, prettier than the vast majority of "pure software" companies.

The venture firm Insight Partners put it sharply: **these companies that doubled down on implementation and service during the platform transition are exactly the ones that persisted while everyone ran the other way**. The secret is that gross margin can climb year by year—as the platform accumulates and thickens, more and more of the implementation work is something the customer can do itself (the Bootcamp model lets a large share of integration work be done on the customer side), and service cost gets continuously diluted. Ugly first, beautiful later; heavy first, light later. (Source: Appendix C.)

The final story of this chapter is one of "betting the right direction." At the end of 2022, when ChatGPT burst onto the scene, everyone was guessing what Palantir would do. Sankar placed a bet that would later be quoted repeatedly: large models would quickly commoditize, and value would concentrate at the two ends—at one end the compute chips, at the other the Ontology (the semantic layer that lets models work safely on enterprise data). He pressed most of the company's engineers onto the AI platform AIP. The result: Palantir's stock rose from single digits when he took over as CTO to around $200; U.S. commercial revenue posted triple-digit growth for several quarters running. The logic of this bet is the flip side of Chapter 1: **the stronger and cheaper the model, the more valuable the engineering that "wires the model into reality"**. (Source: Appendix C.)

The endpoint of the four questions of productization is a closure of the entire FDE business model: **the field is the probe, the platform is the lever, the product is the vehicle of compound interest**. The truer the world the probe detects, the more solidly the platform accumulates capability; the more solid the platform, the farther the probe can go. Once this flywheel starts spinning, you're no longer a "send people to do the work" company—you're a "continuously convert the world's complexity into software assets" company. That is the species with the highest profit margin and the deepest moat since the software industry was born.
