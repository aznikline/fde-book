> "On the wrong problem, all execution is waste."

## 2.1 Anatomy of the POC Purgatory

There is a phrase in Silicon Valley: "POC purgatory." Projects that enter it never come out: not dead enough to be declared failures, not alive enough to warrant further investment. So year after year they sit in the quarterly reports as "ongoing," like a room full of patients hooked to tubes.

MIT researchers performed a systematic autopsy of this graveyard. They distilled five roadblocks on the path to scaling enterprise AI projects, ranked by frequency of appearance:

1. Employees refuse to use the new tools—ironically, the same people use ChatGPT privately every day and love it
2. Concerns about model output quality
3. Poor user experience
4. Lack of executive support
5. Difficulty in change management

Note what is absent from this list: models not smart enough, compute not cheap enough, technology not advanced enough—none of them appear. What kills these projects happens almost entirely at the level of "problem definition" and "organizational reality," not at the technical level.

A detail repeatedly cited in the report deserves to be told on its own. A company spent fifty thousand dollars on a specialized contract analysis tool with an impressive feature list. But one senior lawyer at the company simply refused to use it—she kept drafting contracts with the free ChatGPT. The reason was plain: the bought tool's summaries were too rigid to be customized to her habits. The procurement department's report read "deployed," while the real daily reality was an official system spinning idle and employees routing around it. (Source: Appendix C.) This detail reveals the true cause of the number-one roadblock: it's not that employees are conservative—it's that consumer-grade products have spoiled their taste. People who fly on it at home every day can't stomach "artificial stupidity" in the office.

There is a more piercing contrast: **projects that partner with external specialist vendors have about twice the success rate of those built in-house**. Why do external teams actually win more? Not because they are smarter—because they cannot afford to lose. People who are paid by results bear the cost of defining the wrong problem themselves. One Japanese company's in-house project is a textbook anti-example: the three or four strongest engineers were pulled into a strike team, the demo dazzled, the leadership nodded, but from day one no one could answer "by what standard is this system's goodness measured." Half a year later the project quietly vanished from the briefing materials; no one declared it a failure, it simply died a natural death. (Source: Appendix C.)

This is the first principle of this chapter: **on the wrong problem, all execution is waste; and the density of wrong problems inside enterprises far exceeds imagination**. This chapter covers only one thing: before writing the first line of code, how to make sure you're solving the right problem.

## 2.2 PSF: Finding Problem-Solution Fit

In internet startup methodology there is a core concept called PMF (Product-Market Fit): once the product is right, the market pulls growth on its own. In the world of FDE, the corresponding unit is not "product and market," but "problem and solution"—I call it PSF (Problem-Solution Fit).

The difference is subtle and crucial. PMF asks "does the market want my product," with the viewpoint on the supply side; PSF asks "is this specific problem of the customer worth solving, and can it be solved by our capability," with the viewpoint on the demand side. Inside one enterprise customer there may be hundreds of opportunity points for "what AI can do," but the ones truly worth doing must pass three gates at once.

The first gate, the pain test: is this problem a specific pain of a specific person? Note the two occurrences of "specific." "Improve customer service efficiency" is not a pain point, it's a direction; "the customer service supervisor spends three hours every Monday morning manually aggregating last week's escalation tickets from four systems, when her real job should be analyzing the reasons for escalation"—that is a pain point. McGrew offered a sharper criterion: go solve one of the top five problems the CEO cares about most. The reasoning is brutally practical—only a problem of that magnitude can help you roll over the bureaucratic resistance inside the enterprise (Chapter 3 expands on how to use it to get past the IT gate). Sierra's head of agent engineering, Meurer, states the same principle in another form: take only two kinds of problems—ones that are genuinely hard, and ones that have genuine business impact. Hard without impact is showing off; impact without hard won't fall to you.

The second gate, the economics test: how much is solving this problem worth? Many pain points are genuinely painful but not worth money; projects that can't settle this account won't survive the next budget season. You can do a rough calculation: how many person-hours does this problem eat every week? How much does that convert to in labor cost? How much does one error cost? What could the saved headcount be redeployed to do? Palantir's Bootcamp simply front-loads this gate—the customer must lock down the "core battleground" and give a quantified target before things start: "reduce scheduling conflicts by 30%," "cut inventory turnaround by 15%." A finding widely cited in that report happens to show how easily this gate is skipped: more than half of enterprise AI budgets go to front-office sales and marketing, while the returns concentrate in back-office contract review, procurement, and risk control—these "unsexy" places—because everyone is solving "problems that demo well" rather than "problems that are worth money."

The third gate, the feasibility test: with our current capability and this customer's data reality, how far can we get? This is the gate most easily overwhelmed by enthusiasm. Two questions must be answered on site: where is the data, and in what state? The answer is often worse than imagined—scattered across seven systems, three versions that don't match, and the most authoritative copy sitting in a veteran employee's personal spreadsheet. And, what accuracy threshold does this problem demand? The engineering investment between "99% usable" and "90% usable" differs by an order of magnitude, while many business scenarios in fact find their optimal solution at 90% plus human review—judging this requires not technology but an understanding of business consequences.

Only when all three gates are passed do you touch Problem-Solution Fit. And these three gates must be passed at the customer site—sitting in the headquarters conference room making judgments from second-hand information is the root of all the errors in this chapter.

## 2.3 Refusing the Expensive "POC Purgatory"

In 2025, a Chinese service provider doing enterprise AI delivery wrote a fairly ruthless passage on its website to turn away prospective customers: those whose scenarios aren't yet validated, go join a demo event first; those whose data can be exported in a single spreadsheet, a lightweight service is enough; those who just want to understand AI, use the free way. The last sentence is the sharpest: "FDE is a heavy investment; we'd rather you start later than have you start at the wrong time." (Source: Appendix C.)

This passage is worth every enterprise AI practitioner copying onto the wall. It voices an anti-sales-intuition truth: **turning down the wrong project is the FDE model's most important profitability**.

Why are wrong projects dangerous? Because FDE's cost structure is front-loaded—the best engineers, the most expensive travel, the longest on-site investment, all happen before payment is collected. Once you sink into the swamp, it's not a matter of losing one deal; it's that an entire elite team gets dragged down and the opportunity cost avalanches.

Former Palantir engineer Barry recalls that the company "burned millions of dollars on customer pilots, and many projects' profit margins were literally negative infinity, because we did them for free." The perspective he added right after is the point: Palantir could afford to burn because it invested in pilots as an R&D portfolio—like venture capital, it's fine if most bets go to zero, as long as the ones that hit win it all back. But if you have neither its capital depth nor the mechanism to "convert failed pilots into product assets," then every wrong pilot is pure blood loss. (Source: Appendix C.)

So an FDE team needs a "mechanism for refusal," not just the courage to refuse. Three actionable lines of defense.

- **Line of defense one: every POC must have a "graduation standard."** When each validation project is launched, it must specify in writing: after how many weeks, using what metrics, reaching what values, the project "graduates" into paid deployment; if it doesn't reach them, the two sides part gracefully. Palantir's Bootcamp takes this logic to its extreme—it's not a POC, it's the industrialized replacement for the POC: one to five days, the customer brings real data, a deployable prototype is built on site, executives sign off on the spot. Either see something real in days, or don't start. The reason the traditional POC degenerates into a graveyard is precisely that it is "indefinite in duration, metric-free, and referee-free."

- **Line of defense two:** watch for three high-risk signals. Drawing on practitioners' experience, when two or more of these signals appear, be on high alert. First is "ownership without land"—the project has no clear business owner inside the company, only IT is interfacing; IT cares about compliance and stability, and compliance and stability are never reasons to do a new project. Second is "look but don't touch"—the customer asks you to prove capability first but refuses to provide real data; validation without real data is doomed to produce self-deceiving false positives. Third is "universe-grade requirements"—a customer whose first meeting demands "covering every scenario across the whole company" usually isn't ready to do any single scenario.

- **Line of defense three:** give "no" a graceful exit. Refusal does not mean cutting ties. The best move is to translate "not now" into "when": "The data foundation for this scenario is still three things short; we suggest doing another scenario first, which happens to fill those three gaps, and come back next quarter." Packaging refusal as a roadmap both preserves capacity and maintains the relationship—Chapter 3's "lighthouse customer screening" continues along this line.

## 2.4 Pain Points—The First Prime Mover of Deployment

Where do the right problems come from? Textbooks will tell you: from requirements research. FDE field experience will tell you: **from pain. And pain doesn't appear in the conference room, it only appears at the workplace**.

Palantir wrote this method in blood twenty years ago. Look back at the Iraq battlefield story in section 1.2: soldiers needed a roadside bomb early-warning tool—this requirement could not be elicited in any interview, because soldiers didn't know "you can ask software for this," they thought it was just part of patrol life. The pain point was "seen" by the engineer, not "spoken" by the user. The forward deployed engineer rode along with the patrol, saw with his own eyes the hesitation and fear of the convoy at suspect stretches of road, and only then did that crude map tool that changed the battlefield come into being.

This method has a name in anthropology: "participant observation"; in the Toyota Production System it's called "genchi genbutsu"—go to the spot, see the real thing, get the real situation. FDE turns it into an operable field method, which I call the "shadow work method."

Follow a real user through a real day of his. Not interview him—sit beside him and watch him work: see which systems he opens, which spreadsheets he copies and pastes between, where he frowns, which "official processes" he routes around. OpenAI's FDE team did exactly this on the John Deere project: fly to Iowa, follow agronomists and farmers into the fields, see how they make spraying decisions, see which information actually enters the decision, see how seasonal deadlines govern everything. That oft-quoted practitioner maxim describes the discovery targets of this method: "The hard part is finding the workflow nobody wrote down, the data source people actually trust, and the person who knows why the process is the way it is." Each of these three can only be found on site.

Focus on observing "workarounds," not "processes." The official flowchart tells you how the organization "should run"; workarounds tell you how the organization "actually runs"—every workaround is an unsatisfied pain point screaming. Why do employees insist on exporting data to a spreadsheet and recalculating it? Why is it common knowledge in the department that "for this table, you have to find Old Wang"? Why, even with a data system in place, does someone always manually verify the numbers before the decision meeting? Workarounds are the scars of the organization; under every scar is a systemic failure, and also an FDE opportunity.

Beware of "translated pain points." The requirements you hear, if they are relayed through the customer's IT, procurement, or consulting advisors, lose fidelity with each handoff—IT translates business pain into technical requirements ("we need a data platform"), procurement translates it into compliance clauses ("needs to meet such-and-such standard"). The disasters of traditional software projects often start right here: the vendor is accountable to the translation, not to the pain itself. So your first duty is to bypass the translation and reach the nerve ending of the pain. This is also the core responsibility of "Echo" in Palantir's dual-person model—understanding the customer's "mission" rather than its "requirements": requirements are the second-hand narration of the pain point; the mission is the first-hand source of the pain.

Once the real pain point is found, the next step is to verify at minimum cost: can our solution actually relieve this pain?

## 2.5 Validating Value with "Minimum Viable Deployment"

In internet startup methodology there is a famous concept called MVP (Minimum Viable Product): use the smallest product to validate market demand. The FDE counterpart, I call **MVD (Minimum Viable Deployment)**—use the minimum engineering investment, in the customer's real environment, against a real pain point, to validate one real occurrence of value.

A one-word difference, but the difference is in the bones. The MVP validates "should we build this product," and the judge is the market; the MVD validates "can this solution produce value for this specific customer," and the judge is this specific customer's specific business. A solution validated successfully at ten customers can still fail at the eleventh—the data foundation is different, the organizational inertia is different, the shape of the pain point is different. This is the cruelty of enterprise delivery: **value cannot be inherited, it can only be validated one by one**.

The MVD has three iron rules.

- **Rule one:** real data, no exceptions. Doing validation on "desensitized (sensitive information redacted) sample data" provided by the customer or on demo data you construct yourself is the first brick of the POC purgatory. All the devils hide inside real data: field meanings that don't match the documentation, three-tenths null values, the encoding rules from three years ago, and most fatally—the data itself records the wrong process. - **How this rule lands:** Palantir's Bootcamp writes "the customer must bring their own real business data" into the rules, and the John Deere project put "reviewing hundreds of real operational cases" before modeling. A solution that holds on fake data is just an elaborately crafted piece of self-comfort.

- **Rule two:** shrink the incision, not the ambition. A common mistake is to understand MVD as "a castrated version of the big plan"—cutting away seventy percent of the features and making something that looks like nothing. The right move is to shrink along a different dimension: don't cut value, cut scope. Don't pursue "intelligent customer service covering the whole company," but "cover only the returns-and-exchanges ticket category, but do it end-to-end, with no human intervention"; don't pursue "supply chain optimization for the whole group," but "only handle the scheduling conflicts on this one production line, but genuinely save 20 person-hours every week." Shrink the incision until the value density is high enough—high enough that the business department can see it with the naked eye and spread it on its own. Legal AI company Harvey's expansion path is the textbook of this playbook: instead of rolling out across the whole firm, first punch through one global business group, make the first batch of partners into believers, and expand horizontally after six months of combat.

- **Rule three:** pin down the deadline and force trade-offs. The validation cycle of an MVD should be counted in "weeks," not "months." Palantir's Bootcamp is one to five days, Sierra's fastest publicly reported go-live case is four weeks, Decagon's typical deployment is four to eight weeks. The point of the deadline is not speed, but to force both sides into honest trade-offs: whatever can't demonstrate value within these few weeks is not yet the core value. A six-month "minimum validation" almost inevitably regrows into a want-everything big project—that's the POC purgatory breaking ground once again.

### Bootcamp: The Industrialization of MVD

Palantir's AIP Bootcamp, launched in 2023, deserves to be broken out on its own as the benchmark for "industrializing MVD"—it is currently the only MVD pipeline that has been validated at scale.

First, the data: starting from fewer than a hundred pilots in 2022, the number of sessions has doubled year over year; media tracking shows the cumulative completed sessions have long passed a thousand, with an average of nearly 6 per day during the 2025 peak; the traditional enterprise software sales cycle (nine to twelve months) was compressed to weeks; U.S. commercial revenue grew 137% year over year in Q4 2025, and the company publicly attributed almost all of that growth to it. (Source: Appendix C.)

Next, the process, which breaks MVD into five standardized moves. Day 0, preparation: both sides lock down an extremely focused core battleground—"optimize scheduling on a given production line," "reduce inventory turnaround days"—rejecting all grand narratives. Day 1, integration: wire up the customer's existing systems, pull out the isolated data, build an initial Ontology model. Days 2 to 3, construction: FDEs and the customer's technical staff work back-to-back writing code and configuring rules, wiring the large model into the business flow, producing automated workflows that can execute real actions. Days 4 to 5, demo and sign-off: the output is not a report but a living software interface, on which the business executives lay their own hands, watching AI give recommendations based on their own company's data—after the shock, they go straight into commercial negotiation.

The Bootcamp's brilliance lies in solving three classic MVD problems at once: the real-data problem (the customer brings it themselves), the deadline problem (capped at five days), and the judge problem (executives use it themselves). It also incidentally solves a deeper problem—trust. Letting the decision-maker personally operate a system built on their own data beats a hundred-page feasibility report.

How fast do the contracts after Bootcamp get signed? Palantir disclosed a set of real rhythms on an earnings call—so fast that peers might not believe it: a large healthcare company attended a Bootcamp in December and signed a five-year, $26-million-a-year agreement five weeks later; a global bank first signed a $2-million initial contract one month after the pilot, then expanded it four months later into a three-year, $19-million-a-year contract; the pharmacy chain Walgreens first piloted at 10 stores, where in-store operating efficiency rose 30%, then rolled it out to 4,000 stores within eight months, with AI-driven end-to-end workflows automatically handling about 384 billion decisions a day that were originally made by humans. (Source: Appendix C.) These numbers answer "what happens after the MVD": validated projects don't grow slowly, they scale in leaps—within five days the customer has already seen the value with their own eyes; what remains is just the commercial process.

Of course, replicating this model has a threshold: it requires a mature platform base behind it, otherwise even the environment can't be stood up in five days. For teams without a platform, an executable simplified version is the "two-week sprint validation": week one, enter the site, connect the data, define the metrics; week two, build a prototype that solves only a single point but runs on real business, and demonstrate it to the business side on the weekend and decide on the spot whether to advance or retreat. The form can be trimmed; the iron rules cannot.

## 2.6 Should You Accommodate the Customer's Existing Environment Early On

During the MVD phase there is a divergence that almost every project runs into: the customer's existing technology environment—that pile of twenty-year-old systems, the little tools built by individual departments, and the strict security and compliance boundaries—how much should our solution accommodate it?

Both camps have a point. The "accommodate camp" says: validation in the customer's real constraints is the only real validation. The "refactor camp" says: doing deep adaptation for a legacy environment about to be replaced is wasting precious validation time on engineering that's destined to be thrown away.

FDE practice offers a middle path: be compatible with legacy systems at the data layer, never accommodate them at the architecture layer—I call this "read old, write new."

At the data layer, deeply accommodate the legacy environment. Wherever the customer's data lives, read it from there—even if it's in an old mainframe, in a spreadsheet on a shared drive, in some ancient system's private interface. Financial-industry legacy mainframes and the heterogeneous systems across hundreds of clinics in healthcare have always been core battlegrounds of deployment. There are no shortcuts to data compatibility, because data is the precondition for validating value, and data will never move house to accommodate your architecture. The good news is that this layer of work is itself being transformed by AI: field mapping that used to require manual interpretation, cross-system hauling, and fetching from old systems without interfaces can now be largely handed off to agents—browser agents, for example, can simulate human operation to pull data from old systems that have no interface. Integration costs are dropping by an order of magnitude. (Source: Appendix C.)

At the architecture layer, absolutely refuse to be a parasite of the legacy environment. The validation-phase system should run within its own controllable boundary, interacting with legacy systems through interfaces, not writing code into the legacy systems. There are three reasons: the validation-phase solution itself has more than a fifty-percent chance of being overturned and rewritten, and the deeper the parasitism, the greater the waste; writing into legacy systems must go through the customer's change-management process, with cycles measured in months, fundamentally clashing with the MVD's weekly rhythm; and keeping a "can-evacuate" posture is itself a negotiating chip and a posture of honesty—the FDE finishes and leaves, a parasite never can.

At the process layer, defer to people's habits, not the system's habits. This is the one most easily gotten backwards by technical teams. The technical environment can be stubborn; people's habits must be deferred to. If the business user's core actions happen in spreadsheets and email, then the MVD's interface should appear in a spreadsheet plugin and in email, not require the user to log in to a brand-new portal. OpenAI's deployment at BBVA, which entered from the ChatGPT interface that 120,000 employees were already using rather than starting from scratch, is a model of deferring to habit. Remember the cause of death in section 2.1: employees' reluctance to adopt new tools ranked first among the five roadblocks. **The biggest enemy of a new system is not the old system, it's the old habit**.

## 2.7 "Actions Speak Louder Than Words" User Research

At the end of this chapter, pull the lens back to the source of the methodology and talk about the divide between FDE-style user research and traditional research.

The creed of traditional research is "ask": questionnaires, interviews, focus groups. The creed of FDE is "look" and "do"—actions speak louder than words. There are three reasons, each building on the last.

First layer: customers don't know what they want. This is not disparagement, it's a cognitive law. Faced with a brand-new category—intelligence-analysis software in 2004, AI agents in 2025—users have no frame of reference to describe requirements. Palantir's demo loop works precisely because it doesn't ask "what do you want," but says "here's what I built, you tell me what's wrong with it." People's judgment about "what's wrong" is far stronger than their imagination about "what they want." Finding faults in a given solution is a human talent; describing an ideal solution from scratch is a human shortcoming.

Second layer: what customers say and what they do are two different things. The lawyer example in section 2.1 is the best proof: surveyed about "willingness to use specialized legal AI," the procurement department will tell you the intent is strong—after all, they just spent fifty thousand dollars on the tool; but observe the lawyers' actual behavior, and they're drafting contracts with ChatGPT. In the enterprise context, "saying" is contaminated by too many factors: political correctness, politeness toward the vendor, defense of one's own role. Only behavior doesn't lie. The shadow work method observes behavior, the MVD measures behavior—both rest on the foundation that "doing is truer than saying."

Third layer: the highest-quality research happens in shared labor. In an interview, the customer is a "research subject," guarded and performing; working side by side, the customer is a colleague, relaxed and real. During the two or three days when the FDE and the customer's technical staff write code back-to-back in a Bootcamp, the information density exchanged exceeds any formal research—the customer will casually say in a debugging gap "actually we never trust this field" or "on paper this process goes through the system, but in reality it's still done by phone." These words would never appear in a formal interview, because they look "informal." Yet they are precisely the intelligence on which deployment success or failure turns.

There's one more new variable from 2026 worth adding here: interviews themselves are being reshaped by AI. Ding Xindong, CTO of Tezign, shared the practice of no longer sending out structured questionnaires, but using an Agent to conduct autonomous interviews across the entire company—tailoring the conversation to different scenarios and to different people's actual pain points, and finally aggregating them into a global diagnostic map of "what stage each product line is at, what its pain points are, and what approach suits its advancement." Questionnaires elicit answers; interviews surface pain points—AI brought the cost of the latter down. (Source: Appendix C.)

The three layers taken together, FDE's research methodology can be condensed into one sentence: **use the shadow work method to find pain points, use Minimum Viable Deployment to validate the solution, use shared labor to win the truth**.

At this point, the right problem is locked and the value is preliminarily validated. The next battleground is turning the validated single-point value into a real contract, and a relationship that truly begins—how to win the customer.
